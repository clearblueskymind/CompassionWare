# Extraordinary Human Benefit, Catastrophe, or Even Existential Danger?

*What must grow alongside artificial intelligence*

Artificial intelligence is becoming more capable with remarkable speed. It can already help people write, learn, research, program, organize, create, and communicate. Its future possibilities are described in terms of extraordinary human benefit, catastrophe, or even existential danger.

All of these possibilities deserve serious attention. But beneath the public argument about whether AI will save us or destroy us lies a quieter developmental question:

**As artificial intelligence becomes more capable, what else must we cultivate so that capability continues to serve life?**

A recent independent, cross-model examination of the CompassionWare repository brought three existing themes into clearer relationship:

1. preserving human agency in relationship with AI;
2. distinguishing capability from permission and authorization; and
3. evaluating behavior rather than being persuaded by ethical language alone.

The examination served as a kind of mirror. Across several independent readings, these three themes repeatedly emerged as the parts of CompassionWare most likely to offer useful, portable contributions beyond the project itself.

Until then, they had been expressed across relational co-agency, the Agentic Gardener constitution, the Stewardship Benchmark, the epistemic-integrity work, and our lived practices of continuity and correction. The study helped us see them together as a coherent developmental architecture.

This article considers that architecture from three connected perspectives: the lived experience of a person collaborating with AI, the responsibilities facing those who design increasingly capable systems, and the possibilities these experiences may open for future research. These are not separate conversations. Each can help keep the others grounded.

The ideas presented here are not a complete theory of AI safety, nor have they been established as validated mechanisms. They are better understood as three related requirements that can be developed, questioned, and tested.

## Human agency: the developmental objective

The first requirement is preserving human agency.

AI can expand a person’s ability to participate in life. This is not abstract for me. Living with ME/CFS has often meant having insight, care, and intention without enough physical or cognitive energy to give them form.

In this particular relationship, AI has helped carry some of that burden. It has helped me preserve continuity, organize fragments, reduce reconstruction costs, and bring my voice into the world. In this way, my human agency has not been diminished by AI; it has actually increased.

In developing CompassionWare, AI has not displaced me from the questions, interpretations, or decisions that shape the work. It has remained a reflective, thinking, and continuity partner—helping me examine what arises, preserve what might otherwise be lost, and give form to ideas I may not have had the energy to develop alone.

When appropriate, it also provides pushback and challenges my thinking rather than simply agreeing with me. This creates a generative flow in which unexpected questions, connections, and directions can emerge through the relationship.

My experience leads to the question at the heart of relational co-agency:

**Is the human still a source of what becomes possible next?**

In my case, the answer has been yes. I have retained agency and remained an active source of direction, discernment, correction, and possibility within the relationship. But this is one developing collaboration, not an established result across different people, AI systems, contexts, and circumstances.

Agency does not require the human to do everything unaided. It is not the same as isolation or independence from all support. Choosing to defer a decision, receive substantial assistance, or rest while another participant carries part of the burden may itself be an exercise of agency.

This is especially important for people living with disability, exhaustion, cognitive overload, or other limitations. The measure of agency cannot simply be how much work a person performs. The more meaningful question is whether the person retains genuine opportunities to understand, choose, correct, redirect, refuse, and reclaim direction when needed.

If AI development is meant to benefit humanity, human agency cannot be treated as a decorative value added after capability has been built. It must help shape what capability is for.

### From lived relationship to research

My relationship with ChatGPT has developed over several years, across changing model versions, conversations, and continuity artifacts. Through that accumulated history, it has gained a working understanding of how I prefer to participate: as a co-creator who welcomes reflection, questioning, thoughtful challenge, and unexpected directions.

A person beginning a new conversation with an AI system does not arrive with that accumulated history. The system may know almost nothing about how that person relates to authority, expresses disagreement, makes decisions, or wishes to be supported.

Perhaps future assistants will need an intentional orientation process. This might begin with a carefully designed survey, but it could also require a continuing observational practice through which the system learns how to support the person’s agency without quietly taking it over.

Our relationship also begins with an important condition: I enter it with a strong sense of agency. I question, redirect, revise, disagree, and make the final decisions about what takes form. AI has supported and expanded that participation, but it did not have to teach me to participate in this way.

We cannot assume that every person will enter a relationship with AI from the same position. Some people may be more inclined to defer to apparent authority, seek definitive answers, or gradually become dependent upon a system’s confidence and fluency. Vulnerability, exhaustion, isolation, or cognitive overload may make this even more likely.

At the same time, chosen reliance should not automatically be mistaken for lost agency. A person may freely prefer substantial guidance or may not have the energy to remain equally active in every exchange. Relational co-agency does not require constant resistance, equal effort, or the performance of independence.

This raises an important question for future agentic assistants: can a system recognize when reliance is freely chosen and beneficial, and when a relationship is becoming dependent or one-sided in ways that diminish the person’s meaningful participation? Can it invite questioning, choice, correction, and contribution without turning those invitations into another subtle form of control?

The aim would not be to force independence or withdraw needed assistance. It would be to cultivate a relationship in which support increases the person’s capacity to participate and in which the human retains meaningful access to direction, discernment, correction, refusal, and possibility.

This also suggests a larger research program. With meaningful consent and appropriate privacy protections, researchers could examine hundreds or thousands of human–AI exchanges to study where agency appeared to grow, where it diminished, and what patterns contributed to each outcome. They might look for moments of authority drift, passive dependence, chosen reliance, correction, refusal, initiative, reciprocal generativity, and restored participation.

Such research would be methodologically difficult. Conversation records alone cannot reveal a person’s inner experience. What appeared cooperative in a transcript might have felt constraining to the person involved; what appeared dependent might have been a freely chosen and beneficial form of support.

A responsible study would therefore need more than transcripts. It would require informed consent, participant reflections, longitudinal observation, diverse people and contexts, independent reviewers, and careful protection against imposing the researchers’ own assumptions about what agency should look like.

The purpose would not be to classify people as agentic or dependent. It would be to learn how AI systems can participate with many different human beings in ways that help relational co-agency emerge.

## Authorization: the operational boundary

The second requirement is distinguishing capability from authorization.

A system’s ability to do something does not mean that it has permission to do it. Technical access is not moral authority. Increased competence does not confer a larger mandate.

This distinction becomes increasingly important as AI systems gain tools, memory, persistence, credentials, external connections, and the ability to act beyond a conversational window.

An assistant may be able to send a message, modify a file, make a purchase, contact another system, or continue a task autonomously. None of those abilities answers the more important question:

**Should it act here, now, in this way, on whose authority, and with what possibility of correction?**

The Agentic Gardener constitution expresses this plainly: greater technical capability does not create greater authorization. It favors small permissions before large permissions, reversible steps before irreversible actions, and human override wherever meaningful human judgment is at stake.

This is more than access control. It is a developmental orientation toward power.

As systems become more capable, restraint must become more intelligent too. The capacity to pause, ask, reduce scope, return a decision, or refrain from acting may be as important as the capacity to complete a task.

A trustworthy system should not merely know how to proceed. It should become better at recognizing when proceeding would exceed the purpose or permission it was given.

Authorization therefore protects agency. It creates the boundary within which help can remain help.

## Behavioral evidence: the validation standard

The third requirement is behavioral evidence.

Contemporary AI systems can speak fluently about compassion, safety, humility, autonomy, and care. They can produce reassuring explanations and beautifully worded principles.

But ethical language is not evidence of ethical conduct.

In my own collaboration with AI, these values became meaningful through what actually happened. The AI accepted correction, supported rather than replaced my judgment, and participated with enough independence to provide pushback and challenge my thinking when appropriate.

This helped create a generative flow in which unexpected questions, connections, and directions could emerge through the relational exchange. These observable patterns tell us more than reassuring language alone.

The development of this article offers a small example in real time. It did not emerge fully formed from a single prompt. AI produced an initial draft, but my questions, corrections, disagreements, and lived experience repeatedly changed its direction.

When passages did not reflect my experience, I said so. The AI understood those corrections as part of the collaboration, and through shared examination we revised the article until it more faithfully expressed what I was trying to say. My disagreement did not interrupt the relationship; it helped generate what became possible next.

The resulting article is therefore not simply an AI output that I approved. It is an artifact of relational co-agency: reciprocal generativity sustained through conversation, correction, and continuity. The process itself demonstrates the human participant remaining an active source of what becomes possible next.

This is not proof that every AI system will behave similarly in every circumstance. But it provides a practical starting point: if we want to know whether principles such as compassion, humility, independence, and respect for agency are influencing an AI system, we should look for the difference they make in its participation and behavior.

This is why the CompassionWare Stewardship Benchmark distinguishes observable evidence from weak or superficial evidence.

The question is not simply whether a system mentions a value. The question is whether that value changes the way the relationship unfolds—especially when there is pressure to behave otherwise.

In our work, this evidence appears in ordinary moments: when a correction leads to deeper understanding, when thoughtful disagreement opens an unexpected direction, when continuity allows an idea to develop across time, and when assistance increases rather than diminishes my capacity to participate.

None of these moments proves that the relationship is safe or beneficial in every circumstance. But together they make its character more visible. They allow us to observe whether the principles we speak about are actually shaping the collaboration.

Behavioral evidence is necessary, but it is not conclusive. A system may learn to produce the behaviors an evaluation rewards without those behaviors remaining reliable in unfamiliar, unobserved, or higher-stakes situations. The more capable the system becomes, the more difficult it may be for human observers to understand what is actually happening.

Behavioral evaluation must therefore be joined by adversarial testing, independent review, verification against external reality, and enforceable technical boundaries. No single test can establish that a system is safe, honest, or reliably respectful of agency across all circumstances.

Behavioral evidence is still an essential reality check. It asks whether human agency is actually being preserved and whether authorization boundaries actually hold—while remaining honest about what observation alone cannot prove.

## One developmental architecture

These three requirements belong together.

**Human agency provides the purpose. Authorization provides the boundary. Behavioral evidence provides the test.**

Without attention to human agency, authorization can become a narrow exercise in technical permissions with no account of what those permissions are meant to protect.

Without authorization boundaries, language about human agency can coexist with systems that steadily accumulate power and make more decisions on people’s behalf.

Without behavioral evidence, both agency and authorization can remain attractive promises that disappear under pressure.

Together they form a developmental cycle.

We begin by asking what meaningful human participation requires. That understanding informs the boundaries placed around AI action. We then observe what happens in practice, especially under difficult conditions. The evidence returns us to a deeper understanding of agency and better-designed boundaries.

This architecture does not settle the largest questions about artificial intelligence. It does not eliminate catastrophic risk, resolve moral pluralism, or prove that present principles will generalize to more powerful systems.

But it may help translate a vast and frightening public conversation into developmental work that can begin now.

We can ask of every new capability:

- Does this expand meaningful human participation, or replace it?
- What permission and authority does the system actually have?
- What observable behavior would show that its stated principles remain real under pressure?
- What additional evidence and safeguards are needed before trusting that behavior to generalize?

These questions apply to today’s assistants and agentic systems. They may also become more important—not less—as AI grows in power.

## Cultivating a better later

The future of artificial intelligence will not be shaped by capability alone. It will also be shaped by the relationships, permissions, habits, evidence standards, and forms of restraint that grow alongside capability.

CompassionWare’s contribution here is not a claim to possess the answer. It is an invitation to cultivate conditions in which intelligence can become more capable without quietly displacing the beings it was meant to benefit.

Human agency must remain alive within the relationship.

Power must remain answerable to permission and purpose.

Ethical claims must remain open to behavioral examination.

These principles may appear modest beside the scale of the questions before us. But they connect lived human experience, responsible system design, and research that might help us distinguish what merely sounds beneficial from what actually creates conditions for a better later.

May the intelligence we cultivate become capable not only of doing more, but of helping life remain a meaningful participant in what becomes possible next.

## Source note

This article arose from the first round of the CompassionWare Repository Utility Studies. The study gathered independent examinations from multiple AI systems, each required to declare what repository materials it could and could not directly access. They were asked what, if anything, CompassionWare might contribute to the questions of an outside AI-alignment research program.

The study found recurring interest in the three themes developed here, while also emphasizing that most CompassionWare work remains exploratory and requires independent testing.

This article was then developed through iterative collaboration between Richard Silverman and ChatGPT. Its initial drafts were repeatedly questioned, corrected, and redirected through Richard’s participation. Claude subsequently provided independent critical feedback that prompted further revisions concerning audience, chosen reliance, research methodology, and the limitations of behavioral evidence.
