## About This Guide

You do not need to master AI overnight.

Your presence, judgment, and care still matter.

You can learn alongside your students, one thoughtful step at a time.

This guide expands the eight ideas introduced in the **AI + Learning: A Quick Vocabulary Reference for Teachers** infographic.

The infographic is the quick reference.

This companion is for the moment when you find yourself wondering:

**“What might this actually look like in my classroom?”**

You do not need to use every idea at once.  
You do not need to adopt this vocabulary.  
And you do not need to redesign your teaching around AI.

Many of these ideas are simply good teaching made newly important by technologies that can answer questions, generate writing, explain concepts, solve problems, summarize information, and sometimes perform parts of the learning process for students.

The question is not only:

**Can AI help complete this task?**

It is also:

**What happens to the learner when the task is completed this way?**

---

## A Note About the Examples

The classroom examples in this guide are illustrations, not prescriptions.

Different ages, subjects, students, learning needs, cultures, schools, and technologies will call for different choices.

Use your professional judgment.

The purpose of these ideas is to support that judgment, not replace it.

# 1. 🌱 Learner State

## What it means

**What can this learner understand, retrieve, apply, and carry forward right now?**

A student can participate fluently in an AI-supported conversation without necessarily understanding the material independently.

A polished answer is therefore not always evidence of learning.

Learner State shifts attention away from:

**“Did the student produce the answer?”**

and toward:

**“What can the student actually do with this learning?”**

## Why it matters with AI

Generative AI can make performance look stronger than underlying understanding.

A student may produce a clear paragraph, solve a difficult problem, or discuss an unfamiliar concept with substantial AI assistance.

That may be useful.

But the finished product alone does not tell us what the learner now understands or can do independently.

## In the classroom

A student uses AI to help improve an essay.

Afterward, instead of evaluating only the polished essay, you might ask the student to explain:

- what they changed,
- why they changed it,
- which suggestion they rejected,
- and what they would do differently next time.

Or after an AI-supported math problem, ask the student to solve a similar problem without assistance.

The goal is not to catch the student.

The goal is to see what learning has become theirs.

## Watch for

**Output being mistaken for understanding.**

A strong artifact may reflect strong learning, strong assistance, or some combination of both.

## A question to ask

**What can this learner now do that they could not do before?**

# 2. 🎚️ Appropriate Complexity

## What it means

**The right amount of challenge for this learner, in this moment.**

Learning generally needs enough difficulty to require thought without becoming so difficult that meaningful participation collapses.

Appropriate Complexity is not a fixed level.

It can vary by student, subject, skill, day, and situation.

## Why it matters with AI

AI can change the difficulty of a task almost instantly.

It can simplify a reading, supply vocabulary, generate examples, explain a concept differently, provide hints, or perform much of the task itself.

That makes AI potentially valuable for accessibility and differentiation.

It also means we need to notice when assistance has reduced the challenge so much that the learning opportunity disappears.

## In the classroom

A student is struggling with a difficult science passage.

AI might:

- explain unfamiliar vocabulary,
- restate one paragraph in simpler language,
- provide an analogy,
- or ask the student guiding questions.

That may preserve the intellectual task while making it more reachable.

Having AI simply summarize the entire reading and answer all subsequent questions may remove much more of the learning.

## Watch for

**Difficulty becoming either shutdown or disappearance.**

Too much challenge can overwhelm.

Too little challenge can leave very little for the learner to do.

## A question to ask

**What amount of help keeps this student meaningfully in the learning?**

# 3. ⚓ Anchoring Before Advancing

## What it means

**Sometimes learning needs to become stable before more is added.**

New material is not always the next best step.

Students sometimes benefit from retrieving, practicing, explaining, comparing, applying, or revisiting what they have already encountered.

Consolidation can be progress.

## Why it matters with AI

AI makes moving forward extraordinarily easy.

There is almost always another explanation, example, extension, answer, or level of complexity available.

But more information does not necessarily mean more learning.

Students may need time to make existing understanding more durable before AI helps them move farther.

## In the classroom

Before students ask AI to analyze a story's symbolism, you might first ask them to identify important details themselves.

Before AI helps extend a mathematical method into more difficult problems, students might retrieve and apply the foundational method independently.

Before asking AI for a more sophisticated revision of an argument, a student might first explain the argument in their own words.

## Watch for

**Speed being mistaken for progress.**

AI can accelerate the movement through material without necessarily strengthening what remains afterward.

## A question to ask

**Does this learner need something new—or does something already introduced need to become more stable?**

# 4. 🧗 Productive Struggle

## What it means

**Some difficulty is part of learning when support is available.**

Students often learn through attempting, remembering, wondering, revising, making mistakes, trying again, and discovering where their understanding breaks down.

The goal is not struggle for its own sake.

The goal is meaningful engagement with difficulty that can produce learning.

## Why it matters with AI

AI can remove friction very quickly.

Sometimes that is exactly what a learner needs.

But some friction contains the learning.

If AI immediately drafts the paragraph, solves the equation, supplies the interpretation, generates the code, or answers the question, the student may lose the opportunity to develop the capacity the assignment was intended to cultivate.

## In the classroom

Instead of asking AI to write the student's first draft, a student might draft first and then use AI to:

- identify unclear passages,
- suggest questions,
- compare possible structures,
- or provide feedback for revision.

In mathematics, a student might attempt the problem first and use AI afterward to help locate the step where their reasoning went wrong.

## Watch for

**Productive struggle becoming unsupported frustration.**

Difficulty is not automatically educational.

A learner who is overwhelmed, confused without a path forward, or unable to participate may need more support.

## A question to ask

**Is AI helping this student through the struggle—or helping them avoid the learning inside it?**

# 5. 🤲 Calibrated Assistance

## What it means

**Give enough help to support learning without doing the learning for the student.**

The best amount of assistance is not always the maximum amount available.

Nor is the goal to withhold help unnecessarily.

Calibrated Assistance asks:

**What kind of help allows this learner to remain an active participant?**

## Why it matters with AI

AI can provide assistance at many levels:

- a hint,
- a question,
- an example,
- an explanation,
- feedback,
- partial completion,
- or a finished answer.

Those forms of assistance are not educationally equivalent.

A useful AI interaction may therefore involve choosing not only *whether* AI helps, but *how much* it helps.

## In the classroom

Instead of:

**“Write my conclusion.”**

a student might ask:

**“Ask me three questions that will help me figure out what my conclusion should say.”**

Instead of:

**“Solve this problem.”**

the student might ask:

**“Give me one hint about what I should examine next.”**

The AI becomes scaffolding rather than substitution.

## Watch for

**Assistance quietly becoming dependency.**

If students increasingly cannot begin, continue, evaluate, or finish work without the tool, the level of assistance may deserve another look.

## A question to ask

**What is the smallest amount of help that keeps this learner moving and thinking?**

# 6. 👀 Tracking Before Teaching

## What it means

**Notice what is actually happening before deciding what should happen next.**

Teachers already do this constantly.

You notice confusion, confidence, fatigue, curiosity, misconceptions, frustration, readiness, engagement, and changes in understanding.

Tracking Before Teaching simply gives that familiar professional practice a name.

## Why it matters with AI

AI systems often respond immediately to whatever is asked.

But the next requested thing is not necessarily what the learner most needs.

A student may ask for the answer when what they need is a hint.

They may ask for a harder problem when the foundation is unstable.

They may appear confused about content when they are actually overwhelmed by the instructions.

Good AI-supported learning still requires someone to notice the learner.

## In the classroom

Before deciding how AI should help, you might ask:

- What has the student already tried?
- What do they understand?
- Where did they become stuck?
- Can they explain the problem in their own words?
- Are they confused, or simply uncertain?
- Would another explanation help, or would additional information make things worse?

Sometimes the most useful intervention emerges only after listening.

## Watch for

**Responding to the request without understanding the need.**

The first thing a learner asks for may not reveal the actual learning problem.

## A question to ask

**What is happening for this learner right now?**

# 7. 🔎 Verification

## What it means

**Check important AI-generated claims instead of accepting them automatically.**

AI can produce information that sounds confident, detailed, and plausible while still being incomplete, misleading, or wrong.

Fluency is not evidence.

## Why it matters with AI

Verification is becoming part of literacy.

Students increasingly need to know not only how to obtain information, but how to evaluate:

- where it came from,
- whether the source exists,
- whether multiple sources agree,
- what evidence supports the claim,
- what may be missing,
- and how confident they should be.

The goal is not distrust of everything AI produces.

It is appropriate trust supported by evidence.

## In the classroom

If AI provides a historical claim, scientific fact, quotation, citation, or statistic, students might be asked to locate the original source.

Students can compare an AI-generated explanation with:

- a textbook,
- primary source,
- scholarly source,
- trusted reference,
- classroom materials,
- or other appropriate evidence.

They can also ask:

**“What would I need to check before relying on this?”**

## Watch for

**Confidence being mistaken for correctness.**

An AI system may be persuasive even when it is mistaken.

## A question to ask

**What evidence would make this trustworthy?**

# 8. 🎒 Teach What Can Be Carried Away

## What it means

**Favor learning the student can eventually use without the teacher or AI present.**

The learning should increasingly belong to the learner.

Teachers, tutors, books, classmates, calculators, and AI can all provide support.

But one purpose of education is for capacities that initially required support to become increasingly available to the learner themselves.

## Why it matters with AI

AI may become a normal part of students' future lives.

The goal therefore does not need to be complete independence from technology.

But students still benefit from developing capacities that remain theirs:

- judgment,
- questioning,
- explanation,
- verification,
- communication,
- reasoning,
- creativity,
- self-awareness,
- and the ability to decide when assistance is useful.

The question is not simply:

**“Can the student do this without AI?”**

It may also be:

**“What is the student becoming able to carry into their next interaction—with or without AI?”**

## In the classroom

After using AI to revise writing, students might identify two revision strategies they could apply independently next time.

After AI tutoring, students might summarize the idea in their own words.

After using AI for research, students might explain how they decided which claims deserved verification.

After coding with AI, students might explain what the generated code does and identify where they would begin if it failed.

The product may disappear.

The capacity can remain.

## Watch for

**Convenience increasing while independent judgment decreases.**

A tool can make students more capable, but it can also make certain capacities less practiced if those capacities are continually outsourced.

## A question to ask

**What will this learner carry forward after the tool is gone?**

# If You're New to AI in the Classroom

You do not need to hold all eight ideas at once.

One possible pathway is:

## 1. Notice

**Learner State**  
**Tracking Before Teaching**

Begin with the learner.

What do they understand?  
What do they need?  
What is actually happening?

## 2. Balance

**Appropriate Complexity**  
**Calibrated Assistance**

Ask how much challenge and how much help will keep the student meaningfully involved.

## 3. Protect the Learning

**Productive Struggle**  
**Verification**

Preserve opportunities to think, attempt, question, and check.

## 4. Build What Lasts

**Anchoring Before Advancing**  
**Teach What Can Be Carried Away**

Help learning become stable enough to remain useful beyond the immediate interaction.

This is not a required sequence.

It is simply one doorway.

# A Few Classroom Patterns to Notice

Sometimes the easiest way to use these ideas is not to memorize their names but to notice recurring patterns.

### When AI produces the whole answer immediately

Ask:

**What part of the thinking still belongs to the learner?**

### When a student is stuck

Ask:

**Would a hint preserve more learning than a solution?**

### When the AI output looks excellent

Ask:

**What does the student understand well enough to explain, evaluate, or reproduce?**

### When students move rapidly through material

Ask:

**Has the learning become stable, or have we simply moved on?**

### When an AI tool makes something much easier

Ask:

**Is this removing an unnecessary barrier—or removing the learning itself?**

### When you're unsure whether to use AI

Ask:

**What would leave the learner more capable afterward?**

# Good Teaching Still Matters

AI changes some of the conditions of teaching.

It does not make teacher judgment obsolete.

Knowing when to explain, when to ask, when to wait, when to challenge, when to scaffold, when to verify, and when a learner simply needs another way into the material remain deeply human parts of education.

AI may contribute new capabilities.

Teachers still help interpret what those capabilities should serve.

---

# One Question to Carry With You

## Will this next move leave the learner more capable?

Not necessarily faster.

Not necessarily more polished.

Not necessarily finished sooner.

**More capable.**

That might mean more knowledgeable.

More curious.

More discerning.

More confident in something they genuinely understand.

More able to ask a good question.

More able to recognize uncertainty.

More able to work independently.

More able to use AI thoughtfully.

Or simply more able to take the next meaningful step.
