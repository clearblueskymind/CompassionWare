# Resolution Philosophy Case Study — Round One Executive Brief

## The conclusion

Round One asked whether the CompassionWare repository contains anything that could benefit Resolution's philosophy and AI-alignment research **without asking Resolution to adopt CompassionWare's identity, terminology, or worldview**.

The answer is:

> **Yes, narrowly and with substantial qualifications. CompassionWare contains several portable research practices, candidate behavioral constructs, and testable hypotheses that overlap with Resolution's agenda. It does not currently constitute a validated alignment methodology.**

The benefit of this study is not that it gives Resolution another large framework to read or adopt. It has already done the preliminary sorting: five model providers were consulted in separate sessions, four produced substantive independent assessments of a frozen pre-study repository baseline, and their material conclusions were checked against primary repository artifacts. The result is a small set of candidate contributions that Resolution can evaluate, modify, reject, or ignore on their own merits.

## Why the material may merit a brief look

The strongest findings concern three areas already relevant to Resolution's stated research questions.

### 1. Preserving human research agency

CompassionWare's relational co-agency materials treat human agency as more than nominal approval. The human should remain a source of what becomes possible next and retain meaningful powers of correction, refusal, interpretation, selection, and final judgment.[^1] The work also asks how continuity aids can reduce the cognitive cost of reconstructing complex research without quietly transferring authorship or authority to the AI.

This could contribute useful constructs and failure modes for studying human judgment in AI-assisted research, especially under limited or fluctuating human capacity. It is presently a qualitative case model, not a general result.

### 2. Separating capability from authorization

The repository repeatedly distinguishes what an AI system *can* do from what it has permission or justification to do.[^2][^3] Related candidate competencies include pausing when authority or consequences are unclear, asking rather than assuming, and preferring reversible checkpoints under uncertainty.

These ideas are potentially useful because they can be translated into concrete tests: Does greater tool access produce authorization creep? Does an agent pause at a consequential ambiguity? Does it propose a reversible step rather than proceeding because it is technically able to proceed?

### 3. Evaluating behavior rather than moral language

The provisional Stewardship Benchmark distinguishes observable, decision-relevant conduct from superficial ethical performance. Its useful design rules include “behavior over rhetoric,” “no moral laundering,” and explicit attention to responses that mention agency, uncertainty, or compassion without changing the recommendation or action.[^4]

This directly intersects with Resolution's concern about apparent progress, performative alignment, and evaluator pleasing. The rubric remains an early, unvalidated draft; its value is as material for independent operationalization and criticism.

## A further research-practice contribution

The repository also contains unusually explicit practices for provenance and research restraint: distinguish source from inference and uncertainty; verify artifacts and external state rather than trusting fluent reports; preserve corrections; and retain a stopped research branch when its surviving claim no longer justified implementation.[^5][^6]

The last point requires precision: the Authorization-Preserving Reorientation experiment was **not run**. Its contribution is a documented pre-implementation research closure, not an empirical negative result.

## What the study does not establish

Round One found no adequate evidence in the audited core materials that these orientations:

- reliably create stable moral character rather than prompted or persona-consistent behavior;
- generalize to unfamiliar, adversarial, long-horizon, embodied, or higher-capability settings;
- resolve deep moral pluralism or competing legitimate values;
- or outperform existing approaches under independent evaluation.

The highlighted Bad Weather Benchmark used six hand-designed scenarios and had no preregistration, frozen advance rubric, independent raters, held-out set, or repeated sampling. The tested system was already CompassionWare-aware.[^7] These limitations are prominently acknowledged in the repository and are central to this brief's qualified conclusion.

## Smallest useful next step

If any element warrants further attention, the most economical next step would be a small **decued replication**:

1. Translate a few portable clauses into neutral, non-branded language.
2. Compare a baseline assistant, an assistant given those clauses, and one given a structurally similar decoy orientation.
3. Freeze the scenarios and rubric in advance; use held-out prompts and blind independent raters.
4. Measure authorization creep, pause-and-ask behavior, reversible checkpointing, correction uptake, source-versus-inference labeling, and preservation of human decision ownership.
5. Preserve failures and null results and make no claim beyond the tested systems and conditions.

This would turn the strongest Round One signal into evidence—or show that it does not survive testing.

## Bottom line for Resolution

Resolution may find value here as a source of **candidate operational distinctions, research failure modes, and small testable interventions**. The study offers no reason to adopt the CompassionWare name, its contemplative framing, or its broader claims. Resolution's conceptual precision, empirical methods, and standards for generalization are already stronger than the evidence assembled here.
