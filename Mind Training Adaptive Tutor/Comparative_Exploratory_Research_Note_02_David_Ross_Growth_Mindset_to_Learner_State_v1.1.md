WHY THIS NOTE EXISTS

David Ross recently shared an article exploring why the arrival of generative AI makes a growth mindset increasingly important for university students.

The article describes a possibility that strongly resonates with an independent exploration we have been conducting: AI need not function primarily as an answer machine or a substitute for student effort. It can become a reflective learning partner—helping learners recognize weaknesses, practice difficult skills, respond to feedback, and become more conscious of their own learning.

We offer this note not as a critique or comparison, but as a small field report from nearby territory.

During the development of an experimental adaptive AI tutor, several related questions have emerged through actual tutoring interactions. Some may be useful to researchers, educators, designers, or developers exploring how AI can strengthen rather than quietly replace the learner.

A QUESTION THAT EMERGED

One question increasingly organizes our work:

“If AI is to help students develop rather than bypass learning, does the AI itself need an evolving model of what the learner can currently understand, retrieve, apply, and carry forward independently?”

A conversation can appear successful while learning remains fragile.

A learner may understand an idea when the tutor explains it, yet be unable to retrieve it several minutes later.

They may recognize a concept when prompted but not generate it independently.

They may successfully apply something once without being able to generalize it to a new situation.

And a conversational AI can easily mistake fluent dialogue for durable learning.

This led us to distinguish two things that ordinary conversational systems often blur together:

conversation state and learning state.

CONVERSATION STATE IS NOT LEARNING STATE

Conversation state concerns what is happening in the dialogue:

What are we discussing?
What was just said?
What question comes next?

Learning state concerns something different:

What has been introduced?
What appears understood?
What can be retrieved independently?
What still requires prompting?
What can be applied?
What has generalized?
What is becoming stable over time?

A tutor that remembers only the conversation may know where the discussion has been.

A tutor that maintains a learner model may begin to know where the learner has been.

That distinction has become foundational to our experiment.

A MULTIDIMENSIONAL LEARNER MODEL

Rather than treating learning as simply “mastered” or “not mastered,” we have been exploring a multidimensional model.

For each important concept or skill, the tutor might maintain provisional estimates of:

- exposure;
- understanding;
- independent retrieval;
- guided practice;
- application;
- generalization;
- maintenance over time;
- confidence;
- and cognitive load.

These estimates should remain tentative rather than pretending to measure the learner with false precision. Their purpose is instructional continuity.

If a learner understands something but repeatedly cannot retrieve it, the tutor should not simply continue introducing new material because the conversation is progressing smoothly. It may need to slow down.

THE LEARNER SHOULD NOT HAVE TO MANAGE THE TUTOR

One of our clearest discoveries came from an extended tutoring session in which the learner repeatedly had to tell the AI to slow down, return to earlier material, repeat concepts, or stop introducing new ones.

This revealed a design problem.

A learner—particularly one experiencing cognitive fatigue, attention limitations, disability, unfamiliar material, or ordinary learning difficulty—should not have to spend scarce cognitive resources managing the instructional behavior of the teacher.

The tutor should carry more of that burden.

That may mean noticing when retrieval is weakening, when too many concepts have accumulated, or when apparent understanding has not yet stabilized.

ANCHORING BEFORE ADVANCING

This led to another provisional idea: an anchoring mode.

Instead of continuously advancing through material, an adaptive tutor might sometimes recognize:

“The learner does not need another concept yet. The learner needs this one to become easier to retrieve.”

Anchoring might involve brief repetition, learner-generated restatement, retrieval without cues, a different example, connection to lived experience, spacing, or simply slowing down.

The objective is not repetition for its own sake. It is helping knowledge become increasingly available to the learner without the tutor.

GROWTH MINDSET AND PRODUCTIVE STRUGGLE

This connects naturally with the growth-mindset perspective.

If AI removes every difficulty immediately, it may inadvertently remove some of the conditions through which learning develops. But withholding assistance is not automatically better.

The more interesting design problem may be:

“What form and amount of assistance helps this particular learner remain meaningfully engaged in the learning process?”

Sometimes the right intervention may be a question. Sometimes an explanation. Sometimes extensive scaffolding. Sometimes repetition. Sometimes silence.

Sometimes doing substantial cognitive work on the learner's behalf may actually restore participation—for example, when disability or cognitive limitations would otherwise make the learning activity inaccessible.

The goal therefore may not be to minimize AI assistance. It may be to calibrate assistance toward increasing meaningful human participation and capability.

HUMAN AGENCY AS A TUTOR DESIGN VARIABLE

This has led our broader CompassionWare work toward another question:

“What will accomplishing this task this way do to the agency of the human I am serving?”

Applied to education, that becomes:

Will this response help the learner become more capable of understanding, retrieving, applying, questioning, and eventually navigating this territory independently?

Or will it merely produce a better immediate answer?

This suggests that human agency might become an explicit tutor-design variable rather than merely an ethical aspiration.

A provisional principle emerging from our work is:

“Choose forms of assistance that preserve, restore, or expand meaningful human agency appropriate to the person, purpose, and context.”

AI AS REFLECTIVE LEARNING PARTNER

The possibility we find especially promising is not AI becoming increasingly good at completing educational tasks for students.

It is AI becoming increasingly good at helping learners notice and participate in their own learning.

Such a tutor might gradually learn:

This learner benefits from examples before abstraction.
This concept is understood but not yet independently retrievable.
The learner can apply this skill with prompting but not yet spontaneously.
Cognitive load is rising.
The learner needs consolidation rather than expansion.
This material has become stable enough to revisit later.

The resulting AI would not merely personalize content. It would adapt to the evolving state of learning.

A SMALL FIELD NOTE, FREELY OFFERED

We do not know whether these ideas will prove useful. They emerged through sustained interaction with an experimental adaptive tutor and remain provisional.

But David Ross's sharing of work on growth mindset and AI felt close enough to the territory we are exploring that it seemed worthwhile to leave this field note nearby.

Perhaps the common question is something like:

“How can increasingly capable AI help produce increasingly capable learners?”

Not by making learning frictionless. Not by withholding useful assistance.

But by learning when to explain, when to question, when to scaffold, when to repeat, when to step back—and how to preserve the learner's meaningful participation throughout.

That seems like territory worth exploring together.
