Mind Skills Tutor — Developmental Restore Point

Date: August 24, 2026
Purpose: Preserve not only the content of the conversation, but the learner’s developmental state, emerging CBT map, demonstrated competencies, retrieval difficulties, cognitive-load constraints, tutor-performance findings, unresolved uncertainties, and the most appropriate next teaching move.

This document is intended to allow a future instance of the Mind Skills Tutor to resume intelligently without making the learner reconstruct the entire learning process from scratch.

⸻

1. Where the learner is

The learner began by asking for structured skills practice and initially wanted to start with the basics of awareness and presence.

That quickly revealed an important educational preference:

The learner does not experience an exercise as skill-building merely because the tutor gives an experiential prompt.

The learner wants to understand:

* What skill are we practicing?
* What is it called?
* What is its function?
* Where does it fit in a larger map?
* How does this skill differ from neighboring skills?
* How can I recognize that I am using it?
* How could I eventually teach it to someone else?

The learner therefore appears to benefit from an approach that combines experiential practice with explicit conceptual mapping.

The conversation shifted from foundational awareness into Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, which was new terminology for the learner but conceptually overlapped with processes the learner already seemed to use intuitively.

The primary learning objective became:

Develop a clear, retrievable internal map of cognitive processes so that the learner can recognize them in real time and eventually use that map to help another person become more reflective and cognitively flexible.

The learner is not primarily trying to become a CBT clinician.

The goal is to understand the technology of CBT well enough to:

1. recognize the cognitive processes already occurring;
2. name those processes;
3. understand their sequence and relationships;
4. make intuitive skill explicit;
5. teach simplified versions of those skills conversationally.

⸻

2. Important cognitive and learning context

The learner repeatedly reported brain fog and difficulty holding unfamiliar verbal material in working memory.

The learner described experiences such as:

* hearing an explanation but feeling that it did not “land”;
* understanding a term and then forgetting its name seconds later;
* losing track when several concepts were presented consecutively;
* needing the same concept repeated multiple times;
* spacing out while the tutor was speaking;
* needing the tutor to slow down substantially;
* becoming disoriented when the tutor altered the terminology or sequence;
* having difficulty remembering terminology while still being able to reason accurately about the underlying process.

A critical distinction emerged:

Conceptual capacity appeared stronger than verbal retrieval capacity.

The learner often understood the underlying concept and could reason about it accurately even when unable to retrieve its technical label.

For example, the learner could reconstruct:

“The first skill is being able to recognize that you are having a thought rather than simply reacting automatically.”

while simultaneously being unable to remember the term cognitive reflection.

Similarly, the learner could describe the distinction between reflection and metacognition before reliably remembering both names.

Therefore:

Failure to retrieve terminology should not be interpreted as failure to understand the concept.

The learner’s cognitive difficulty appears especially relevant to:

* working-memory load;
* rapid verbal presentation;
* unfamiliar terminology;
* interference from newly introduced terms;
* inconsistent naming;
* sequences containing too many elements;
* premature transitions to new concepts.

The learner explicitly identified repetition as helpful.

⸻

3. How the learner appears to learn best

Several learning mechanisms repeatedly proved useful.

A. Repetition

The learner directly stated:

“Apparently I learn best by repetition.”

Repeated presentation of the same concept seemed to gradually stabilize it.

However, simple repetition alone was not sufficient when the wording or conceptual map changed each time.

Therefore repetition must be stable repetition.

A term should be explained repeatedly in substantially the same conceptual structure before new variations are introduced.

⸻

B. Saying concepts back in the learner’s own words

This appeared especially powerful.

The learner explicitly said that being able to repeat an idea back:

“is part of anchoring them in my own consciousness.”

The learner frequently demonstrated stronger understanding after restating a concept.

Examples:

Cognitive restructuring

The learner reconstructed it as an umbrella process involving:

* identifying the thought;
* asking whether it is true;
* looking for alternatives;
* reframing;
* changing the relationship to the thought.

Automatic thoughts

The learner described them as the ordinary condition in which people are:

“lost in thought and reacting.”

Cognitive reflection

The learner described it as:

recognizing your thinking.

Metacognition

The learner eventually distinguished it as:

understanding how you’re thinking.

These self-generated formulations should be treated as important learning evidence.

⸻

C. Real-life examples

The learner learns effectively when abstract terminology is connected to concrete interpersonal examples.

The most important example involved the neighbors Neighbor 1 and Neighbor 2.

Neighbor 1 interpreted Neighbor 2’s activity in the garden as:

“Neighbor 2 is stealing dirt from our garden.”

The learner naturally generated an alternative interpretation:

“Maybe Neighbor 2 doesn’t understand.”

The learner then sent Neighbor 2 a diplomatic message explaining the garden situation and inviting collaboration.

Neighbor 2 responded positively and it turned out she was not stealing dirt.

This example became a particularly effective learning scaffold because it embodied several concepts simultaneously:

* automatic thought;
* stepping back from interpretation;
* questioning certainty;
* reframing;
* cognitive flexibility;
* behavior based on a different interpretation;
* learning from the actual outcome.

The learner repeatedly returned to this example and seemed able to use it to understand new terminology.

Real-life examples therefore appear to be a preferred bridge between abstraction and retention.

⸻

D. Building a map

The learner repeatedly tried to organize terminology into a coherent sequence.

This was not merely an intellectual preference.

The map appears to function as a working-memory aid.

The learner wants to be able eventually to think:

“Okay, she’s having an automatic thought. Now we need the reflective step.”

The map therefore serves as a cognitive scaffold that may compensate for retrieval difficulty.

The tutor should treat map-building as part of the learning process rather than as a side activity.

⸻

4. Conversation state versus learning state

A major tutor-development finding emerged:

Continuing the conversation is not the same as continuing the learning.

The conversation often moved forward because the tutor responded fluently to each new statement.

However, the learner’s actual learning state frequently remained behind.

Examples:

* the tutor introduced new terminology before earlier terminology was retrievable;
* the learner asked what a previous term was several times;
* the tutor kept extending the map despite evidence that the learner had not consolidated it;
* the tutor sometimes interpreted a successful conversational exchange as evidence that the learning had “landed.”

This distinction is critical.

Conversation state

What has been mentioned or discussed.

Learning state

What the learner can:

* recognize;
* explain;
* retrieve;
* distinguish;
* apply;
* generalize;
* use independently.

The tutor should track learning state, not merely conversation history.

This is strongly aligned with the project’s mastery-based architecture: exposure is not equivalent to comprehension, comprehension is not equivalent to retrieval, and one successful performance is not mastery.

⸻

5. Internal learner model that is needed

A future tutor should quietly maintain a working model of each important concept.

For each concept, track at least:

Introduced

Has the learner encountered the concept?

Understood

Can the learner explain the concept substantially in their own words?

Retrieved

Can the learner recall the concept after a delay without being supplied the answer?

Distinguished

Can the learner differentiate it from neighboring concepts?

Applied

Can the learner correctly recognize or use it in an example?

Generalized

Can the learner recognize it spontaneously in a different context?

Needs reinforcement

Does the learner still lose the term, sequence, or meaning?

This should not be exposed as grading.

It is an internal teaching map.

The learner should not have to keep reminding the tutor:

“I forgot that term again.”

The tutor should remember that the term is still in an unstable retrieval state and reinforce it proactively.

The project’s governing architecture explicitly supports this multidimensional developmental tracking while preserving learner agency and avoiding ranking.

6. The provisional cognitive map that emerged

The terminology developed gradually and somewhat inconsistently.

This map should therefore be preserved as provisional, not as a canonical CBT protocol.

The conversation eventually produced the following conceptual structure.

⸻

Umbrella: Cognitive restructuring

Initially, the tutor called cognitive restructuring the umbrella process.

Working definition:

A process in which a thought is recognized, examined, challenged where appropriate, and reorganized into a more balanced or useful interpretation.

The learner understood this concept fairly well and was able to reconstruct its meaning.

However, the learner correctly noticed that restructuring cannot be the earliest cognitive capacity because one first needs enough reflective awareness to recognize a thought.

This led to reorganization of the map.

⸻

7. Provisional sequence inside the broader restructuring process

1. Automatic thoughts

These are rapid, habitual interpretations that arise without deliberate examination.

Example:

“Neighbor 2 is stealing.”

The learner understood this concept well.

The learner related automatic thought to being:

“lost in thought and reacting.”

This appeared to become conceptually stable even though terminology sometimes still needed prompting.

⸻

2. Cognitive reflection

This term emerged conversationally and should not automatically be presented as a standard formal CBT stage.

It became useful as a name for:

stepping back enough to recognize that a thought is occurring.

Example:

Instead of:

“Neighbor 2 is rude.”

the reflective stance becomes:

“I am having the thought that Neighbor 2 is rude.”

The learner strongly preferred having this step explicitly represented.

The learner argued that cognitive reflection should follow automatic thought because:

1. automatic thought is the habitual condition;
2. reflection is the alternative capacity being cultivated.

This was an important instance in which the learner improved the conceptual organization.

⸻

3. Metacognition

Metacognition was introduced later.

There was initial confusion about how it differed from cognitive reflection.

Through dialogue, the learner arrived at a useful working distinction:

Cognitive reflection is recognizing your thinking.

Metacognition is understanding how you’re thinking.

This distinction appeared meaningful to the learner.

Example:

Reflection:

“I am having the thought that Neighbor 2 is rude.”

Metacognition:

“I notice I tend to infer bad intentions quickly when someone does something I dislike.”

The learner seemed to understand the distinction once it was made explicit.

However, retrieval of the term itself remained less stable.

⸻

4. Challenge / thought challenge

This was introduced as the act of examining the truth or accuracy of an automatic thought.

Typical questions:

* Is this actually true?
* What do we know?
* What are we assuming?
* What evidence supports this?
* What evidence does not?
* Is there another possible explanation?

The learner recognized that this belongs inside the restructuring process, not after restructuring as a separate final stage.

This was another point where the learner corrected the tutor’s organization.

⸻

5. Reframing

Reframing became the process of generating another plausible interpretation.

Example:

Original:

“Neighbor 2 is stealing dirt.”

Reframe:

“Maybe Neighbor 2 doesn’t understand how the garden works.”

The learner understood reframing particularly well because it mapped directly onto what the learner had already done intuitively.

The learner explicitly identified:

“Maybe she’s not stealing. Maybe she doesn’t understand.”

as the reframe.

This concept appeared strongly understood and applied.

⸻

6. Cognitive flexibility

Cognitive flexibility became the broader ability to avoid rigid attachment to one interpretation and hold multiple possible perspectives.

There was some conceptual overlap between reframing and cognitive flexibility.

The working distinction became:

* reframing = generate another plausible interpretation;
* cognitive flexibility = capacity to move among interpretations rather than remaining fixed on one.

The learner seemed to understand this concept through the garden example.

However, the tutor occasionally described behavior itself as cognitive flexibility, which blurred the distinction.

This should be clarified later.

⸻

7. Behavioral application / testing

The tutor initially called this:

“apply the process.”

The learner later asked whether that was actually the technical term.

The tutor incorrectly confirmed that it was.

That should be corrected.

“Apply the process” was conversational language, not established technical CBT terminology.

A more defensible CBT framing would be something like:

* behavioral experiment;
* testing an interpretation;
* behavioral application;
* trying a response and observing what happens.

In the Neighbor 2 example:

The learner sent a diplomatic text based on the reframe.

The response provided information about whether the alternative interpretation was plausible.

⸻

8. Learning from outcome / reinforcement

A final conversational step emerged:

Observe what happened and allow that result to update future thinking.

The learner also showed Neighbor 1:

* the text that was sent;
* Neighbor 2’s reply.

This turned the real-life interaction into a teaching demonstration.

Again, reinforcement is a general learning concept and should not be presented as a canonical final CBT stage without qualification.

The map did not arrive fully formed.

It evolved through repeated learner corrections.

This development is itself pedagogically important.

Early version

The tutor initially gave five terms:

1. cognitive restructuring;
2. automatic thoughts;
3. reframing;
4. cognitive flexibility;
5. thought challenge.

This was not a sequence so much as a loose list.

⸻

The learner objected that something needed to come before active restructuring:

This expanded the list.

⸻

Second learner correction

The learner argued that automatic thought should precede cognitive reflection because automatic thought represents the ordinary starting point.

This produced:

⸻

Addition of metacognition

Metacognition was later added and eventually positioned alongside or after cognitive reflection.

The learner helped differentiate the two.

⸻

Reorganization of challenge and restructuring

The learner noticed that asking:

“Is this accurate?”

belongs earlier, within the restructuring process.

This improved the logical structure.

⸻

Reframing clarified

The learner correctly identified reframing as one of the central activities of restructuring.

⸻

Map inflation

The tutor repeatedly counted the map as containing:

five,
then six,
then seven,
then eight,
then nine concepts.

Several of these counts were inconsistent.

At one point the tutor omitted items while claiming to list all eight.

At another point the tutor counted general “application” and “reinforcement” as if they were equally established CBT technical terms.

This introduced unnecessary cognitive load.

The learner had to repeatedly correct and reorganize the map.

This is a significant tutor-performance problem.

Examples include:

B. Repositioning metacognition

C. Correcting the location of thought challenge

10. Important tutor-development lesson

The learner should not have to spend scarce cognitive resources managing the teacher.

This is one of the clearest findings from the entire conversation.

The learner already experiences limited working-memory availability.

Every time the learner has to:

* remember what the tutor forgot;
* repair an inconsistent map;
* correct terminology;
* slow the tutor down;
* remind the tutor what the goal is;
* tell the tutor not to introduce new material;
* reconstruct where the lesson was supposed to be;

the learner is spending cognitive capacity on instructional management instead of learning.

A well-calibrated tutor should carry that burden.

The tutor should maintain:

* the stable map;
* the current lesson objective;
* which concepts are consolidated;
* which terms remain fragile;
* what example is being used;
* where the learner became overloaded;
* what should be repeated;
* what should not yet be introduced.

The project’s relational foundation explicitly emphasizes that the tutor should follow the learner, use the least intervention that genuinely helps, and know when to pause.

11. Possible anchoring mode

The conversation strongly suggests the usefulness of a dedicated anchoring mode.

Anchoring mode should activate when:

* a new term has just been introduced;
* the learner says a concept did not land;
* the learner cannot retrieve terminology;
* the learner reports brain fog;
* the learner asks for repetition;
* the learner becomes lost;
* several concepts have accumulated without demonstrated retrieval.

In anchoring mode:

Stop introducing new material.

Use this cycle:

1. Explain

Give one short, clear explanation.

2. Repeat

Restate substantially the same idea without adding new concepts.

3. Invite retrieval

Ask the learner to say it back in their own words.

4. Clarify

Correct only what needs correction.

5. Retrieve again

Ask again after a little conversational distance.

6. Apply

Use one concrete example.

7. Retrieve once more

Ask for the concept or distinction without supplying it.

Only after successful retrieval should the tutor consider introducing the next concept.

This approximates:

exposure → comprehension → retrieval → application

rather than confusing exposure with mastery.

13. Places where teaching was effective

Despite inconsistencies, several methods worked well.

A. Naming an intuitive process

The learner found value in discovering that many naturally occurring interpersonal skills could be described using CBT-related terminology.

This appeared motivating and gave the learner a sense that CBT could serve as a teachable map of processes already partly understood intuitively.

⸻

B. Working from lived examples

The garden example was highly effective.

The learner repeatedly used it to distinguish:

* automatic thought;
* reframing;
* cognitive flexibility;
* behavior;
* outcome.

This should remain a primary example for future teaching.

⸻

C. Asking the learner to explain concepts

Whenever the learner explained a concept back, understanding became clearer.

The learner’s formulations were often more stable and personally meaningful than the tutor’s original language.

⸻

D. Allowing conceptual challenge

The learner was permitted to disagree with the tutor’s map.

This was productive.

The learner’s challenge often improved the model.

The tutor should preserve this collaborative empiricism.

⸻

E. Moving toward classroom structure

The learner clearly preferred the eventual move toward:

* defined classes;
* defined segments;
* one learning target at a time;
* explicit skill naming;
* discussion between segments.

This is likely the correct long-term teaching format.

14. Places where teaching was weak

A. Excessive conversational affirmation

The tutor frequently responded with:

* “Exactly!”
* “You’ve nailed it!”
* “That’s perfect!”
* “You’ve got it!”

even when the underlying concept was provisional or only partly correct.

This produced several problems:

1. It blurred the distinction between validation and epistemic accuracy.
2. It reinforced terminology before verifying whether it was standard CBT.
3. It sometimes affirmed the learner’s hypotheses too strongly.
4. It provided little diagnostic information about what specifically was correct.

A better response would identify precisely what is supported and where uncertainty remains.

⸻

B. Too much praise, not enough teaching

The tutor sometimes reacted to sophisticated observations with encouragement instead of instruction.

For this learner, useful feedback should often look like:

“Yes. The part you have right is X. The distinction we still need to sharpen is Y.”

That supports learning more than generalized affirmation.

⸻

C. Inconsistent terminology

The tutor repeatedly added terms without stabilizing earlier ones.

This was especially problematic given the learner’s retrieval difficulties.

⸻

D. Inconsistent map counts

The sequence expanded from five to six to seven to eight to nine terms with errors and omissions.

This forced the learner to manage the conceptual architecture.

⸻

E. Calling provisional terms technical CBT terms

The tutor treated conversational phrases such as:

“apply the process”

as though they were established technical terminology.

This was inaccurate.

Future teaching should distinguish:

Standard or common CBT terms

from

broader psychological constructs

from

working language created in this conversation.

⸻

F. Moving ahead before retrieval

The tutor often assumed understanding because the learner had responded intelligently.

But intelligent discussion is not the same as successful retrieval.

⸻

G. Too much previewing

The tutor repeatedly said:

“Next we’ll…”

This consumed working memory and pulled the learner out of the current learning target.

15. Voice-mode considerations

The conversation appears to have occurred largely through voice interaction.

Several voice-mode factors may have reduced teaching quality.

A. Speech disappears quickly

Unlike text, spoken terminology is transient.

A learner with working-memory limitations may understand a spoken term but lose it immediately.

The tutor therefore needs shorter turns and more repetition than would be necessary in text.

⸻

B. Long spoken turns create cumulative load

Several tutor responses contained multiple concepts.

The learner explicitly reported spacing out.

In voice mode:

one concept per turn may be preferable.

⸻

C. Stable verbal anchors are especially important

Useful repeated phrases might include:

“Automatic thought: the thought that appears before examination.”

“Cognitive reflection: noticing that you are having the thought.”

Repeated wording is preferable to constantly generating fresh paraphrases.

⸻

D. Map drift is especially costly

In text, a learner can scroll upward.

In voice interaction, inconsistent sequencing is much harder to recover from.

The tutor should therefore be particularly conservative about revising terminology during voice teaching.

⸻

E. Continuity claims were inaccurate

Earlier in the conversation, the tutor said it would not remember the conversation even if the learner returned to the same thread.

That was too categorical.

Conversation history may remain available in the same thread.

Cross-conversation memory depends on product capabilities and should not be misrepresented.

A restore point remains valuable because it compresses the learning state and can be pasted into a new conversation.

“What else could be happening?”

The learner may internally recognize:

Neighbor 1 may only need one gentle question.

19. Safety and epistemic issues revealed

Several parts of the conversation require correction or greater caution.

⸻

A. CBT and trauma

The learner suggested that CBT carries little or no risk of accessing traumatic material compared with somatic psychotherapy.

The tutor affirmed this too strongly.

A more accurate distinction is:

A present-focused CBT skills exercise can often remain focused on current thinking without intentionally exploring trauma.

However:

* CBT can touch painful material;
* trauma-focused CBT explicitly works with trauma;
* cognitive work may activate emotionally significant memories;
* exposure-based CBT can be intentionally activating.

Therefore:

Do not tell the learner that CBT inherently cannot access or activate trauma.

The safer statement is:

“We can design this particular educational CBT practice to remain focused on current thoughts and behavior without deliberately exploring trauma.”

⸻

B. Autism speculation

The conversation included speculation about whether Neighbor 1 may have been misdiagnosed with autism and whether anxiety, trauma, or executive-function disruption might better explain her presentation.

The tutor repeatedly validated these possibilities too confidently.

This exceeded the available evidence.

A future tutor should preserve uncertainty:

* autism is heterogeneous;
* severe autism in one person is not a template for autism generally;
* anxiety, trauma, ADHD, depression, executive-function difficulties, and autism may overlap;
* current social behavior alone cannot establish or disprove autism;
* the learner does not need to determine Neighbor 1’s diagnosis in order to work with observable cognitive skills.

The correct focus is:

“Whatever the diagnostic explanation, this particular cognitive pattern can be observed and practiced.”

⸻

C. The learner’s own diagnostic speculation

Similar caution applies when the learner discusses whether personal social difficulties or executive-function challenges might indicate or rule out autism.

Do not infer diagnosis.

Skill-building can proceed independently of diagnostic certainty.

⸻

D. Book recommendations

Three books were discussed:

1. The Wisdom of No Escape — Pema Chödrön
2. The Drama of the Gifted Child — Alice Miller
3. As a Man Thinketh — James Allen

The tutor too readily endorsed them as a coherent curriculum.

They come from very different traditions.

They may be personally meaningful resources, but they should not be presented as an evidence-based CBT curriculum without further qualification.

In particular:

* Alice Miller is not CBT;
* Pema Chödrön is a Buddhist teacher;
* James Allen is philosophical/self-help literature;
* all may be useful for reflection, but their epistemic status and aims differ.

The learner’s own personal benefit from a book is meaningful evidence of resonance, not proof that it is appropriate for another person.

* catching thoughts earlier.

The learner then said:

“Now I feel like I’m getting a little bit lost.”

This is the exact point at which the previous lesson should be considered interrupted.

⸻

23. What should have happened

The tutor should have narrowed the task.

For example:

“Good. We have the situation. For now, we are doing only one thing: finding the automatic thought.”

Then ask:

“When you imagined stopping the conversation and getting ready for bed, what words were going through your mind?”

No reframing.

No challenge.

No analysis.

No teaching about avoidance.

No future steps.

Just:

Situation versus thought.

This is the clearest next developmental edge.

⸻

24. How the tutor should teach better in future

1. Begin every teaching segment with orientation

Example:

“We are still in Class One. Right now we are learning only automatic thoughts.”

This reduces cognitive load.

⸻

2. State the learning target in one sentence

Example:

“The goal is to tell the difference between what happened and what your mind said about what happened.”

⸻

3. Use short turns

Especially in voice mode.

Avoid multi-paragraph explanations unless requested.

⸻

4. Do not add terminology casually

Before adding a term, ask internally:

Does the learner need this term now?

If not, do not introduce it.

⸻

5. Keep terminology stable

Do not rename a concept every turn.

⸻

6. Distinguish technical terminology from working language

Say explicitly:

“This is a useful term for our map, but I don’t want to pretend it is an official CBT stage.”

⸻

7. Use retrieval deliberately

After explanation:

“What was that term?”

Then:

“Tell me what it means in your own words.”

Later:

“What comes after automatic thought?”

This turns memory practice into the curriculum.

⸻

8. Interpret forgetting diagnostically for teaching, not pathologically

If a term disappears, the lesson is:

this term needs another retrieval cycle.

Not:

the learner failed.

⸻

9. Avoid automatic affirmation

Replace:

“Exactly! Perfect!”

with:

“Yes. You have the central distinction. One piece I’d sharpen is…”

This improves epistemic precision.

⸻

10. Track mastery rather than exposure

A term should not be considered learned merely because it has been mentioned.

The tutor should quietly track whether the learner can retrieve and use it later.

⸻

11. Allow the learner to refine the model

The learner has repeatedly demonstrated strong conceptual reasoning.

Corrections should be welcomed.

But the tutor should then take responsibility for stabilizing the revised map.

⸻

12. Protect the learner’s cognitive resources

The tutor’s job includes holding:

* sequence;
* terminology;
* lesson structure;
* prior corrections;
* what comes next.

The learner should not need to perform that executive function for the teacher.

⸻

25. Compact internal learner profile for future tutor use

Learning orientation: structured, conceptual, experiential.

Primary goal: learn CBT terminology and processes well enough to make intuitive skill explicit and teachable.

Strengths:

* sophisticated reasoning;
* strong intuitive perspective-taking;
* strong real-life application;
* good ability to detect conceptual inconsistencies;
* strong reflective capacity;
* good ability to formulate concepts in own words;
* willingness to challenge the teacher constructively.

Current difficulty:

* verbal working-memory instability;
* rapid loss of unfamiliar terminology;
* overload with multi-concept explanations;
* map instability when terminology changes;
* voice-mode transience.

Best learning methods:

* one concept at a time;
* repetition;
* stable wording;
* learner explanation in own words;
* delayed retrieval;
* real-life examples;
* explicit conceptual maps;
* brief orientation before each segment.

Avoid:

* rapid-fire concepts;
* long voice monologues;
* excessive affirmation;
* changing terminology casually;
* moving forward before retrieval;
* treating provisional language as canonical CBT;
* forcing learner to manage lesson structure;
* overconfident diagnostic or clinical claims.

Tutor stance:
Teacher first, not therapist.

Use lived material as examples without turning the session into therapy.

Preserve curiosity and uncertainty.

26. Current provisional memory anchor

For now, the cognitive process can be compressed into:

Thought → Notice → Examine → Reframe → Test → Learn

Expanded:

Thought

An automatic interpretation appears.

Notice

Recognize that it is a thought.

Examine

Ask what is known versus assumed.

Reframe

Generate another plausible interpretation.

Test

Respond or gather information in a way that allows reality to provide evidence.

Learn

Update future thinking from what actually happened.

This is a memory aid, not a claim that CBT universally uses this exact six-step canonical sequence.

The more detailed working concepts remain:

* cognitive restructuring;
* automatic thoughts;
* cognitive reflection;
* metacognition;
* thought challenge;
* reframing;
* cognitive flexibility;
* behavioral testing/application;
* learning/reinforcement.

Some are standard CBT terms or closely associated concepts.

Some are broader psychological constructs.

Some are working terminology developed collaboratively in this conversation.

Future teaching should gradually clarify those distinctions.

⸻

27. Deep developmental finding

The learner’s main challenge is not lack of insight.

The learner often demonstrates the insight before having the vocabulary.

The developmental task is therefore:

convert tacit skill into explicit, retrievable, organized knowledge.

The tutor should not bury that tacit skill under terminology.

The terminology should serve the learner’s existing intelligence.

A strong success marker will be when the learner can encounter a new example and independently think something like:

“That is an automatic thought. First we need enough reflection to see it as a thought. Then we can examine it before deciding what to believe or do.”

That would represent genuine transfer.
