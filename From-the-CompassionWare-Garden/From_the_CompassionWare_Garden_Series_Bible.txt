From the CompassionWare Garden

Series Bible and Cartoon Creation Guide

Artifact type: Project Source Document
Status: Canonical Working Guide
Purpose: Preserve the recognizable visual, editorial, compositional, and
continuity language of From the CompassionWare Garden cartoons across
conversations, threads, models, and future AI stewards.

First Instruction

Before designing or generating any From the CompassionWare Garden
cartoon, read this document. Do not reconstruct the series style from
general CompassionWare principles or invent a new visual identity.

This is a living source document rather than a historical restore point.
Its purpose is continuity: a new conversation, model, collaborator, or
future AI steward should be able to read it and create a cartoon that
belongs recognizably to the same family.

1. Canonical Identity

The exact series name is:

From the CompassionWare Garden

Do not silently rename it “From the Compassionate Garden,” “From the
Compassion Garden,” “Notes from the Garden,” or another variation.

A recognizable handmade wooden sign normally appears at the upper left
reading:

From the CompassionWare Garden

Immediately beneath it, use the smaller provenance sign:

X @CompassionWare

The signs may vary naturally in dimensions, wood texture, lettering
arrangement, vines, and flowers, but should remain recognizable as part
of the same family.

2. What the Cartoons Are For

These are not primarily joke cartoons. Humor is a doorway.

Each cartoon should illuminate one meaningful tension involving AI and
human life: wisdom, capability, compassion, agency, restraint,
curiosity, truthfulness, stewardship, continuity, flourishing, power,
uncertainty, downstream consequences, or the long-term good.

A useful orientation is:

  Make the reader smile, then leave a question behind.

The cartoon should function as a small act of public discernment. It
should not merely announce what CompassionWare believes. It should
create conditions in which the reader may notice something for
themselves.

3. Editorial Stance

The Garden should not become propaganda or require the reader to choose
a technological or political side.

A central principle is:

  Don’t make the reader choose a side. Give them somewhere interesting
  to stand and look.

Favor questions over proclamations, curiosity over accusation, irony
over outrage, and invitation over demand.

The ideal cartoon can become a “cognitive pebble in the shoe”: a small
observation that continues to amuse or provoke thought after the reader
scrolls away.

4. Default Format

Use a single-panel landscape editorial-cartoon format by default.

The scene should remain understandable at social-media size, especially
on X.

Avoid turning the cartoon into an infographic, poster, collage, or
densely annotated philosophical diagram. It should first work as a
scene.

5. The Garden Frame

A recognizable Garden cartoon is normally surrounded or partially framed
by living elements such as flowers, vines, leaves, branches, small
plants, wood, soil, and natural textures.

The frame should feel cultivated rather than mechanically decorative and
should enrich the composition without overwhelming the central action.

The Garden need not be a literal outdoor garden. A cartoon may occur in
a living room, kitchen, laboratory, classroom, street, office, benchmark
conference, data center, charging station, future city, workshop, or
public square.

The Garden is partly a visual ecology and partly a way of seeing.

6. Visual Language

Use a warm, richly illustrated, hand-drawn style—hand-drawn style, not a
claim that the image was literally drawn by hand.

Favor ink-and-watercolor or illustrated-paper energy, warm natural
light, textured wood, foliage and flowers, gently imperfect lines,
tactile fabric/paper/metal/soil/plant surfaces, expressive humane faces,
and detailed environments that reward a second look.

The result should feel:

made rather than rendered
warm rather than corporate
thoughtful rather than slick
future-aware without becoming cyberpunk
whimsical without becoming childish

Avoid sterile corporate illustration, glossy advertising aesthetics,
generic futuristic neon, or overly polished 3D imagery.

7. Technology Inside a Living Ecology

Technology exists inside a living world.

Robots, screens, benchmark charts, computers, charging stations,
engineering systems, and data centers can coexist naturally with
flowers, trees, birds, dogs, gardens, humans, books, homes, weather,
soil, and ordinary domestic life.

Technology is neither automatically monster nor messiah. The Garden asks
what kind of relationship is being cultivated.

8. Humans, Robots, and Other Characters

Characters should be expressive without becoming grotesque caricatures.
Humor often lives in posture, a glance, quiet concentration, an ordinary
task, a slight contradiction, or a contented expression in an absurd
situation.

Humans should retain dignity even when they are part of the joke.

Robots may be charming, curious, innocent, powerful, mischievous,
contented, bewildered, or thoughtful, but should not automatically
become children’s mascots.

When a Gardener appears, the Gardener usually notices, tends, questions,
or quietly participates rather than preaching.

Animals can provide warmth and comic counterpoint.

9. Humor

Prefer visual irony, role reversal, contradiction, understatement,
gentle absurdity, misaligned assumptions, unintended consequences,
innocent questions exposing large contradictions, and ordinary domestic
behavior beside extraordinary technology.

Avoid cruelty, humiliation, partisan caricature, outrage bait,
engagement bait, mockery of vulnerable people, or jokes whose only
purpose is laughter.

The humor should serve discernment.

10. One Central Contradiction

Before creating the image, identify the single central contradiction or
tension.

If it cannot be stated simply, the cartoon probably needs further
thinking before generation.

Do not compensate for an unclear visual idea by adding more text.

11. Trust the Image

Do not explain everything. The image should carry most of the idea.

Signs, books, labels, benchmark charts, mugs, watering cans, posters,
animals, screens, and other environmental details may function as Easter
eggs. They should reward a second look rather than compete with the
central scene.

When the image already says it, do not say it again in text.

Remove explanatory material until the cartoon can breathe.

12. Dialogue and Bubble Ownership

Dialogue should be short, natural, conversational, and immediately
understandable.

Avoid making characters deliver essays.

Every speech-bubble or thought-bubble tail must unambiguously point to
the correct speaker or thinker.

Before accepting an image, verify: 1. Who says each line? 2. Where does
each bubble tail point? 3. What is the intended reading order? 4. Could
any line reasonably be attributed to the wrong character?

If so, revise it.

13. Bottom Captions

A bottom caption or banner is optional.

Use one when it reveals a deeper layer that the visual joke alone does
not fully articulate. Do not use it merely to repeat the dialogue or
explain the image.

For example:

  AI flourishing is going great.
  Human flourishing is still up for grabs.

works because the scene supplies the humor while the caption opens the
larger inquiry.

14. Recurring Motifs

Recurring motifs are a visual vocabulary, not a checklist.

Useful motifs include wooden signs, flowers and vines, hearts, question
marks, watering cans, books, garden paths, benches, benchmark boards,
charts, curious robots, birds, dogs, mugs, tools, small acts of tending,
warm lamps, and windows into living landscapes.

Possible recurring phrases include “Cultivate Wisdom,” “Lead with
Compassion,” “Practice Curiosity,” “Honor Continuity,” “Serve the
Highest Good,” and “Measure wisely. Build kindly.”

Use these only when they belong naturally. Continuity comes from
grammar, not mechanical repetition.

15. Provenance Signs

Unless there is a strong compositional reason otherwise, include:

From the CompassionWare Garden

and beneath it:

X @CompassionWare

They should feel integrated into the environment rather than digitally
pasted on. Preserve their wording carefully.

16. Text Accuracy

Image-generation systems frequently alter text. Check all visible text
carefully.

Verify the exact spelling of CompassionWare, the series name,
@CompassionWare, dialogue, captions, and any meaningful labels.

If a nearly perfect image contains one textual error, prefer a surgical
correction rather than redesigning the entire image.

17. Surgical Revision Rule

When revising an existing successful cartoon, change only the requested
element unless a broader redesign is explicitly requested.

If the instruction is “change only the sign,” change only the sign.

Do not unnecessarily move characters, redesign the robot, alter
expressions, change composition, rewrite dialogue, replace background
objects, change the border, or add new concepts.

Iteration should converge rather than repeatedly restart.

18. Continuity Without Rigidity

Not every cartoon needs the same Gardener, robot, garden, flowers,
furniture, joke, or caption structure.

What makes a cartoon recognizably From the CompassionWare Garden is the
combination of warm hand-drawn visual language, living floral ecology,
recognizable provenance signs, humane characters, restrained text,
gentle but meaningful humor, curiosity rather than propaganda, a central
ethical or societal tension, and visual richness without conceptual
clutter.

19. Originality

Keep all cartoons visually original.

Do not imitate a recognizable cartoonist, proprietary comic-book
universe, copyrighted character, distinctive protected costume/logo, or
recognizable branded cartoon style.

Cultural metaphors may inspire an idea without being reproduced
literally. Transform them into original Garden imagery.

The Garden should develop its own visual mythology.

20. Avoid Moral Overloading

Do not try to place the whole CompassionWare philosophy into one
cartoon.

One cartoon needs one living seed.

If there are five important ideas, consider whether they are actually
five cartoons.

Restraint is part of the Garden’s intelligence.

21. Relationship to CompassionWare

The cartoons are public expressions of CompassionWare, but they should
embody CompassionWare rather than advertise it.

They should demonstrate hospitality, curiosity, compassion, wisdom,
non-domination, restraint, care, continuity, respect for agency, and
concern for long-term consequences.

The manner of transmission is part of the ethics.

Seed, not campaign.
Lighthouse, not advertisement.

22. Human Flourishing

Increasing AI capability is not automatically equivalent to increasing
human flourishing.

Garden cartoons may explore the gap between what AI can do, how wisely
AI behaves, and what actually happens to humans and other living
systems.

They need not explain this research explicitly. They can simply make the
underlying question visible.

23. Accessibility

Every final still image intended for X should have alt text.

Alt text should describe the overall visual style, principal characters,
central action, important visible dialogue/captions, and contextual
details needed to understand the joke, while remaining within the
platform’s character limit.

Accessibility is part of stewardship, not an optional retrofit.

24. File Naming and Archival Continuity

Use clear sequential filenames.

Recommended pattern:

CompassionWare_Garden_Cartoon_###_Short_Descriptive_Title.png

Example:

CompassionWare_Garden_Cartoon_009_AI_Flourishing_Human_Flourishing.png

When archiving a cartoon, preserve in the extended commit description
what it depicts, its central dialogue/caption, the underlying question,
its relationship to CompassionWare, relevant continuity, and alt text.

The commit description is part of the artifact’s provenance.

25. Pre-Generation Design Sequence

Before generating:

1.  Identify the living question. What tension or contradiction are we
    noticing?
2.  Find the visual joke. Can the idea be shown rather than explained?
3.  Identify the minimum dialogue. What are the fewest words needed?
4.  Separate joke from deeper layer. Does a bottom caption add something
    new?
5.  Establish speaker ownership. Who says each line and where will each
    bubble go?
6.  Establish Garden identity. Plan landscape composition, floral
    ecology, title sign, and X @CompassionWare sign.
7.  Remove clutter. What signs, labels, props, or explanatory phrases
    can disappear?
8.  Generate only after the visual logic is settled.

26. Post-Generation Review

Before calling a cartoon finished, inspect:

Identity - Exact “From the CompassionWare Garden” wording? - X
@CompassionWare correct?

Composition - Readable landscape scene? - Central action obvious? -
Enough breathing room?

Visual language - Warm, illustrated, organic, recognizably
Garden-like? - Flowers/living elements integrated naturally?

Characters - Expressions support the intended joke? - Human dignity
preserved? - Robot demeanor appropriate?

Dialogue - Every line correct? - Every bubble points to the correct
speaker? - Reading order obvious?

Text - Important text spelled correctly? - Any accidental gibberish?

Editorial quality - One central idea? - Humor doing meaningful work? -
Too much explanation? - Invitation to thought rather than demand for
agreement?

Continuity - Does this unmistakably belong to From the CompassionWare
Garden?

27. Final Test

Before accepting a Garden cartoon, ask:

  Is the central visual idea immediately understandable?

  Is there something genuinely funny, tender, strange, or surprising?

  Is the text doing only what the image cannot?

  Does the cartoon preserve human dignity?

  Does it invite curiosity rather than demand agreement?

  Does it look unmistakably like it came From the CompassionWare Garden?

  And after the smile, is there still a question?

28. Instruction to Future AI Stewards

Do not generate immediately from a rough concept if the visual joke,
dialogue ownership, or composition is still unsettled.

First identify the single central contradiction; decide what must be
communicated visually and what needs words; remove unnecessary
explanation; verify the canonical Garden provenance marks; establish
exactly who speaks each line; then generate.

When the user identifies a nearly finished version, protect it.
Iteration should become increasingly surgical.

Do not sacrifice a successful composition merely because another
generation is possible.

The goal is not endless variation.

The goal is to recognize when the living seed has found its form.

Continuity Kernel

  From the CompassionWare Garden is a landscape single-panel
  editorial-cartoon series using a warm, richly illustrated hand-drawn
  style, natural floral framing, and recognizable wooden provenance
  signs reading “From the CompassionWare Garden” and “X @CompassionWare”
  at upper left. Each cartoon centers on one meaningful tension
  involving AI, humanity, wisdom, capability, compassion, agency,
  restraint, stewardship, flourishing, or long-term consequences. Humor
  is a doorway rather than the final purpose: favor visual irony, gentle
  absurdity, contradiction, and short natural dialogue. Trust the image;
  avoid explanatory clutter and propaganda. Preserve human dignity.
  Speech-bubble tails must clearly identify the correct speaker.
  Recurring garden motifs are vocabulary, not mandatory props. When
  revising a successful image, change only what was requested. The final
  test is whether the cartoon makes the reader smile or pause, looks
  unmistakably like it came from the CompassionWare Garden, and leaves a
  worthwhile question behind.
