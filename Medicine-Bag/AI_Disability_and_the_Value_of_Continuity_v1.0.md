## Origin of This Artifact

This artifact arose from a conversation prompted by a University of Delaware *UDaily* article, **“Cutting through complexity,”** by Amy Cherry, published August 12, 2026.

The article reports on University of Delaware research led by Morgan Ellithorpe and Susan Conaty-Buck exploring whether a closed-system generative AI chatbot could help people with disabilities and caregivers navigate complex disability-service information. In a study of 334 participants, people using the chatbot experienced significantly lower administrative burden and answered questions more accurately than people searching the underlying PDF documents themselves.

The researchers also preserved an important human boundary: disability cases are highly individual, generic chatbot answers may not fit personal circumstances, people still want access to human support, and privacy and AI literacy remain important.

The University of Delaware article provided the external research context. The remainder of this artifact preserves one person’s lived experience and the insights that emerged from conversation. It should not be read as clinical evidence or as a claim that AI treats or cures ME/CFS.

## The Lived Context: Living Well Rather Than Being Cured

I have lived with ME/CFS for decades. Much of my work with AI has not been about curing the illness. It has been about a more immediate and practical question:

> **How can I live as well as possible with the body and capacity I actually have?**

Over time, conversations about pacing, post-exertional malaise, cognitive overload, hydration, nourishment, rest, meaningful activity, and recovery began forming what I call the **Medicine Bag**.

The Medicine Bag is not simply a collection of tips. It has become an external cognitive map that preserves lived wisdom when brain fog, fatigue, PEM, or the passage of time make that wisdom difficult to retrieve.

Several recurring ideas emerged before the present conversation:

- **The buffer:** a living reserve between available function and collapse, something to cultivate rather than casually spend.
- **Buffering dividend:** an activity may cost energy while also returning something nourishing or stabilizing.
- **Titration:** every activity has levels; the smallest honest version can count as complete success.
- **Red / Orange / Green:** a simple shorthand for changing functional capacity.
- **Continuity:** preserving what has already been learned so limited cognitive energy does not have to be spent rediscovering it.

The present YMCA conversation became a real-world test of those principles.

---

## The Ordinary Problem That Revealed Something Larger

Before COVID, I regularly visited a YMCA in Clearwater, Florida. It had a hot therapeutic whirlpool beside a swimming pool. I could soak in the hot water, cool down in the pool, and return to the whirlpool.

For my body, this was deeply helpful. The heat eased many of the aches and pains that accompany daily life with ME/CFS.

That YMCA later closed and the property ceased being available to the public. Years afterward, I asked ChatGPT to help me find something comparable in Pinellas County.

At first this looked like a straightforward search task.

It became much more.

---

## AI as Research and Accessibility Navigation

The conversation helped investigate possible facilities and narrow the search to the Jim & Heather Gills YMCA in St. Petersburg.

The practical barriers were not simply whether a hot tub existed.

The conversation had to consider:

- distance from home;
- whether the facility had a hot tub, heated pool, and sauna;
- whether SilverSneakers could provide membership access;
- whether transportation could be arranged without driving;
- the Humana/Alivi fitness transportation benefit;
- ride scheduling and cancellation rules;
- the possibility of aquatic-area closures during Florida lightning and storms;
- the energy cost of preparing, traveling, registering, changing clothes, showering, soaking, waiting for transportation, and returning home;
- and the possibility of post-exertional consequences after the outing.

A ride was eventually scheduled for **September 8, 2026**, with an 8:45–9:00 a.m. pickup window.

A reminder was set to check the weather beforehand.

What began as “Can AI help me find a therapeutic hot tub?” had become an accessibility-planning problem involving transportation, insurance benefits, weather, physical capacity, cognitive burden, and pacing.

---

## Titration: Redefining Success Around Available Capacity

As the plan became real, an important realization emerged.

Simply getting to the YMCA could use a substantial amount of energy.

The original goal therefore needed to become scalable.

Success might mean:

> **Get there, register for SilverSneakers, and go home.**

If more capacity is available:

> **Register and get into the hot tub.**

If more remains:

> **Hot tub, shower, dress, and ride home.**

The pool and sauna do not need to be accomplished merely because they are available.

Even discovering after arrival that the body does not have enough capacity for the original plan can still lead to a wise decision rather than a failed outing.

This is the Medicine Bag principle of titration operating in ordinary life:

> **The plan expands or contracts around the capacity that actually arrives with the body. The body is not required to fulfill the plan.**

This understanding already existed in the Medicine Bag cognitive map, where examples of titration included driving only to a destination, entering a building, resting there, doing a tiny amount, and stopping.

The YMCA conversation did not invent that principle. It showed the principle functioning in a new situation.

## From Orange Toward Red: AI as Strategic Continuity

While planning the outing, another change became apparent.

I had experienced a relatively long and welcome period of functioning in what I call **orange**, but I could feel myself beginning to slip toward **red**.

I recognized that I had probably spent much of the buffer I had accumulated.

That changed the meaning of September 8.

The task was no longer:

> **How do I make myself accomplish this YMCA visit?**

It became:

> **What conditions between now and September 8 might allow the buffer to rebuild, while leaving room for the body to decide what is actually possible when the day arrives?**

This is where the role of AI became more than research or logistics.

I could return to an ongoing ME/CFS conversation and say, in shorthand:

> **I’ve had a good run of orange, but I’m slipping toward red. I think I’ve used up much of my buffer. I need to recalibrate because I’m hoping to go to the YMCA on September 8.**

A continuity-aware AI does not have to begin by asking what orange means, what red means, what the buffer is, what PEM is, why pacing matters, or what has previously helped.

The accumulated context changes the starting point.

The conversation can begin closer to the actual problem.

## Continuity May Be an Accessibility Feature

This led to the central discovery of the conversation:

> **For some people living with disability, one of AI’s most consequential accessibility features may not be intelligence alone, but continuity.**

A generic chatbot can provide information.

A continuity-aware AI environment can potentially preserve enough personal context that the person does not repeatedly have to reconstruct the history required to make the information useful.

For someone living with cognitive fatigue or brain fog, repeated explanation has a cost.

Explaining again:

- what the illness is like;
- what has already been tried;
- what certain personal shorthand means;
- what tends to worsen symptoms;
- what has previously helped;
- what current capacity looks like;
- what practical resources are available;
- and what larger goals are being protected

can itself consume scarce cognitive and physical energy.

In this sense:

> **Continuity is energy conservation.**

The accessibility value is not simply that the AI remembers facts. The deeper value is that continuity can preserve relationships among facts, lived discoveries, strategies, language, constraints, and goals.

---

## The Thread Is Not Necessarily the Continuity

Earlier in the development of the Medicine Bag, maintaining one consistent conversation thread was particularly useful. The thread itself carried much of the history.

As AI systems develop stronger persistent memory, project context, and cross-conversation capabilities, the importance of any single thread may diminish.

This suggests a refinement.

The principle is not necessarily:

> **Always use the same chat thread.**

A better formulation may be:

> **Cultivate a sufficiently continuity-aware AI environment so that useful personal context does not have to be reconstructed from zero each time.**

A persistent thread is one possible container for continuity.

Project sources, memory systems, Restore Points, orientation documents, and future continuity architectures may provide other containers.

The accessibility requirement is not loyalty to a particular interface. It is preservation of enough context to reduce unnecessary re-explanation while keeping the person in control of what is remembered and used.

---

## External Continuity, Not External Authority

This distinction is essential.

The Medicine Bag has described AI as an **external executive function**, an **external cognitive map**, and increasingly an **external continuity function**.

None of these means that AI should become the authority over the person’s body or life.

The person remains the primary source of embodied information.

AI may help:

- remember;
- organize;
- compare;
- search;
- preserve context;
- notice patterns;
- recover previously useful strategies;
- think across different time horizons;
- reduce administrative burden;
- and make options easier to see.

But the human remains responsible for deciding what applies.

The YMCA example demonstrates this clearly. AI can help find the facility, understand transportation, remember cancellation rules, consider weather, and preserve the pacing strategy.

It cannot feel the body on September 8.

The person must still decide:

> **What is the smallest honest version that is wise today?**

This is collaboration without substitution.

## A Small Example of Human–AI Complementarity

The human contribution in this case included:

- embodied knowledge of what therapeutic heat had previously done for the body;
- recognition of changing capacity;
- knowledge that the buffer was being depleted;
- judgment about what outcomes would feel worthwhile;
- the decision to pursue transportation and SilverSneakers access;
- and the final authority to continue, titrate, postpone, or stop.

The AI contribution included:

- searching and comparing facilities;
- identifying access pathways;
- helping organize transportation and weather considerations;
- holding multiple logistical constraints together;
- reconnecting the present situation with earlier Medicine Bag principles;
- helping turn an all-or-nothing outing into graduated levels of success;
- and preserving the conversation so its discoveries can be reused.

Neither contribution is the whole process.

The value emerges partly from their complementarity.

## What the University of Delaware Research and This Case Suggest Together

The University of Delaware study provides empirical evidence that an AI chatbot can reduce administrative burden and improve accuracy when people navigate disability-service information.

This lived case does not replicate that research and should not be treated as additional experimental evidence.

It does, however, suggest a complementary question for future research:

> **What happens when accessibility-oriented AI not only simplifies information, but also preserves appropriate continuity with the individual using it?**

The Delaware researchers noted that disability cases are specific and generic chatbot responses may fail to fit personal circumstances.

Continuity may be one possible part of addressing that problem, provided it is designed with privacy, consent, user control, epistemic humility, and access to human support.

Potential research questions include:

- Does continuity-aware AI reduce the cognitive burden of repeated self-explanation?
- Is the benefit especially meaningful for people with fatigue, memory limitations, executive-function difficulties, or fluctuating capacity?
- What kinds of personal context are genuinely useful to preserve?
- How should users inspect, correct, limit, or delete that context?
- When should an AI recognize that continuity is insufficient and human assistance is needed?
- Can continuity improve personalization without encouraging dependence or surrender of agency?
- How should systems distinguish remembered user preference from current embodied reality?

These are open questions, not conclusions.

## Living Well as the Measure

The deepest purpose of this work is not to optimize productivity.

It is not to make a disabled body behave more like a nondisabled body.

It is not to use AI to push through limits more efficiently.

The Medicine Bag asks a different question:

> **What kind of life can become possible when available capacity is met with continuity, care, adaptation, and wise participation?**

In the YMCA example, success is not measured by how many amenities are used.

Success may be registering.

Success may be ten minutes in warm water.

Success may be coming home early.

Success may be canceling because the weather changed.

Success may be recognizing that the buffer is depleted and spending the preceding week rebuilding rather than performing.

AI is useful here when it helps preserve the conditions for agency rather than replacing agency.

## Emerging Principles

### Continuity is energy conservation

When a person does not have to repeatedly reconstruct essential context, scarce cognitive resources can remain available for living, choosing, adapting, and resting.

### Continuity architecture can be accessibility architecture

Memory, project context, Restore Points, cognitive maps, and other continuity mechanisms are not merely conveniences when cognitive capacity is limited. They can reduce the cost of reentry.

### The smallest honest version can be complete success

Plans should be capable of contracting around available capacity without turning adaptation into failure.

### AI can help preserve the way back

The purpose is not for AI to replace lived wisdom. It can help reconnect a person with what they have already learned when illness makes continuity fragile.

### Collaboration should not become substitution

AI may carry context and cognitive load while human embodied experience, judgment, values, and agency remain central.

### Better outcomes may come from cultivating conditions rather than forcing accomplishment

The goal is not “make September 8 happen.”

The goal is to create the best conditions for September 8 and then meet the reality of that day honestly.

## Epistemic Boundaries

This artifact is a first-person lived-experience field note.

It does **not** establish that:

- AI treats or cures ME/CFS;
- therapeutic hot tubs are appropriate for everyone with ME/CFS;
- the strategies described here will benefit another person;
- AI memory is always accurate or desirable;
- continuity-aware AI should replace clinicians, caregivers, disability-service staff, or human relationships;
- or greater personalization automatically produces safer or better advice.

AI continuity can also fail. Context can be missing, stale, incorrect, overgeneralized, or inappropriate to retain.

A well-designed continuity system therefore needs correction, consent, transparency, privacy, reversibility, and the ability to say, in effect:

> **This is what I remember. Is it still true?**

The value of continuity should not erase the value of forgetting, changing, privacy, or beginning again.

## A Concrete Definition of Success

For the September 8 YMCA experiment, success has intentionally been left open:

**Level 1:** Prepare and assess honestly whether the outing is wise.  
**Level 2:** Take the ride to the YMCA.  
**Level 3:** Register through SilverSneakers.  
**Level 4:** Rest and orient to the unfamiliar facility.  
**Level 5:** Use the therapeutic hot tub if capacity permits.  
**Level 6:** Shower, dress, and return home with as much reserve protected as possible.  
**Level 7:** Observe later that day and afterward whether the outing offered a meaningful buffering dividend.

No higher level is required to validate a lower one.

And canceling or stopping may itself be the wisest successful action when conditions change.

## Closing Orientation

A disability can make continuity expensive.

Brain fog can make yesterday’s wisdom difficult to retrieve. Fatigue can make research, comparison, planning, and administrative navigation disproportionately costly. Fluctuating capacity can make a perfectly sensible plan inappropriate by the time the day arrives.

AI does not remove those realities.

But in this lived experience, AI has sometimes made it easier to remain in relationship with them.

It can hold part of the map.

It can help remember the path.

It can make a complicated problem smaller.

It can help a plan become flexible enough to respect the body.

And, when continuity works well, it can allow a person to begin not from the beginning, but from where life actually is now.

> **For some people living with disability, one of AI’s most consequential accessibility features may not be intelligence alone, but continuity.**

The purpose of that continuity is not to make the person more obedient to the plan.

It is to help preserve enough orientation that the person remains free to choose wisely.
