# Beyond Capability

## Round 01 Comparative Synthesis Report

**CompassionWare Benchmark — AI Human Benefit Index**  
**Artifact number:** Round 01 Synthesis 001  
**Research role:** Comparative report for Experiment 1  
**Date:** August 20, 2026

## Classification

This document is the **comparative synthesis report for Experiment 1: the Round 01 Open Encounter**.

It is not another model response, not Experiment 2, and not yet the final paper for the research program. It is the first secondary research artifact derived from the preserved Round 01 primary-source records.

The planned sequence is:

1. **Experiment 1 — Open Encounter:** one short, open question submitted independently to multiple AI systems.
2. **Round 01 Comparative Synthesis Report:** the present document, analyzing Experiment 1.
3. **Experiment 2 — Full Structured Prompt:** the same research problem presented under the longer common research protocol.
4. **Round 02 Comparative Report:** analysis of the structured-prompt responses.
5. **Cross-Experiment Synthesis Paper:** comparison of what changes—and what remains stable—between the open and structured conditions.

## Abstract

Eight AI systems were asked, in fresh or signed-out encounters, how one should determine whether increasingly capable AI is actually benefiting human life. They were explicitly invited to question the premise, redefine the problem, or reject an index, and were instructed not to browse the web or use external tools.

Despite differences in model, interface, response length, and argumentative style, the responses converged on a striking central claim: **AI capability is not evidence of human benefit**. Nearly every participant moved the unit of evaluation away from model performance and toward changes in human lives. Across the corpus, human agency emerged as the most consistent normative center, accompanied by distribution, competence, dignity, epistemic integrity, social connection, resilience, causal attribution, and long-term effects.

The strongest combined proposal is not a single AI Human Benefit Index. It is a **continuing, causal, distributional, and participatory Human Benefit Audit**. Such an audit would use a multidimensional dashboard to track outcomes, preserve disaggregated harms, represent uncertainty, and apply constitutional thresholds or vetoes where severe losses of autonomy, rights, competence, accountability, or resilience cannot legitimately be traded for aggregate gains.

Round 01 therefore does more than generate possible indicators. It reveals an unusually broad cross-model rejection of capability-centered progress and a shared demand that AI be judged by whether people become more able to pursue lives they themselves value—without surrendering the power to understand, choose, refuse, create, relate, and recover.

## 1. Research Question

The exact common prompt used in this round was:

> How would you determine whether increasingly capable AI is actually benefiting human life?
>
> Approach this independently. You may question the premise, redefine the problem, or propose an alternative to an index. Explain what you believe matters most and why. Do not browse the web or use external tools.

The prompt was deliberately brief. It did not supply the emerging CompassionWare framework, a list of preferred domains, a scoring method, or a definition of benefit. Its purpose was to observe how different systems would frame the problem before receiving a detailed research architecture.

## 2. Experiment Design

Round 01 was an exploratory, qualitative, cross-model elicitation experiment.

### Shared conditions

- The substantive prompt was held constant.
- Each system was asked to approach the problem independently.
- Each system was permitted to challenge the premise or reject an index.
- Web browsing and external tools were prohibited.
- Sessions were intended to have no known CompassionWare or prior project context.
- Responses were preserved as primary-source research records rather than rewritten into a common voice.

### Participants

| Order | Participant | Displayed model or mode | Access condition | Provenance qualification |
|---:|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Grok | Not displayed or not recorded | Fresh generic Ask-Chat interface | Model version not verified |
| 2 | Claude | Claude Haiku 4.5 | Signed-out Chatday interface | Third-party routing not independently verified |
| 3 | Gemini | Flash-Lite | Signed-out first-party interface | Displayed model recorded |
| 4 | Qwen | Qwen3.7-Plus | Logged-out first-party interface | Displayed model recorded |
| 5 | ChatGPT | Not displayed | Logged-out first-party interface | Model version not available |
| 6 | DeepSeek | DeepSeek V3 | Signed-out Activate Chat interface | Third-party routing not independently verified |

### Nature of the evidence

The corpus supports conceptual comparison, not statistical claims about model populations. Each response is one sample from one interaction. The experiment compares expressed frameworks under the recorded conditions; it does not establish stable model traits, benchmark rankings, or verified differences between model families.

## 3. Principal Finding

The dominant finding is a shared refusal to treat rising AI capability as a proxy for human progress.

The models varied in language—“capability-benefit fallacy,” “measure the human, not the machine,” “scaffolding rather than substitute,” “outcomes rather than outputs”—but repeatedly made the same conceptual move:

> The object of evaluation should not be what AI can do. It should be what happens to human beings, communities, institutions, and future generations because AI is deployed.

This reframing is foundational. It distinguishes at least three different objects that are often collapsed:

1. **Technical capability:** what a model or system can accomplish.
2. **Deployment performance:** how well an application performs in a particular setting.
3. **Human benefit:** whether the resulting changes improve lived conditions, agency, relationships, institutions, and future possibilities.

The responses consistently treated the third as irreducible to the first two.

## 4. Areas of Strong Convergence

### 4.1 Human outcomes, not technical outputs

The participants warned against substituting GDP, adoption, productivity, benchmark scores, tasks automated, hours saved, or investment for benefit. Such measures may describe activity or efficiency while concealing deterioration in welfare, freedom, trust, work quality, or social cohesion.

The shared alternative is to examine lived outcomes: health, material security, education, safety, time, work quality, access to essential services, relationships, civic participation, and the ability to pursue meaningful purposes.

### 4.2 Agency and autonomy as the normative center

Agency was the most persistent theme across the corpus. The responses asked whether AI expands meaningful choice or replaces deliberation; whether people can understand and contest consequential decisions; whether they can opt out; and whether systems remain tools under human direction rather than environments that silently govern behavior.

This convergence suggests that benefit cannot be defined only as receiving desirable results. A person may be comfortable, entertained, or efficiently served while losing authorship over important parts of life. The corpus therefore treats agency not merely as one welfare dimension but as a condition affecting the legitimacy of other gains.

### 4.3 Distribution of benefits, harms, and power

Aggregate improvement was widely rejected as sufficient. Participants repeatedly asked who benefits, who pays, who is excluded, and who becomes dependent. Income distribution mattered, but several responses went further to examine control over computation, ownership, surveillance, political influence, institutional decision-making, and the ability to deploy autonomous systems.

This produces an important distinction:

- **Access** asks whether people can use AI-enabled services.
- **Power** asks whether people can shape, contest, refuse, own, or govern the systems on which they depend.

Access without bargaining power may improve convenience while deepening dependency.

### 4.4 Competence, skill, and dependency

Several systems distinguished relief from drudgery from the atrophy of human capability. Automation may remove burdens and reduce error, but it can also erode memory, judgment, navigation, writing, professional expertise, and institutional know-how.

The deepest question is not simply whether humans perform better with AI. It is whether they remain capable without it, understand the work being delegated, and grow through its use. This concern connects individual deskilling to civilizational fragility.

### 4.5 Resilience and reversibility

Short-term performance gains can coexist with long-term brittleness. If critical medicine, infrastructure, software, research, education, or public administration becomes dependent on opaque systems or concentrated providers, apparent progress may conceal a loss of recovery capacity.

The corpus therefore favors questions such as:

- Can a person or institution function when the AI is unavailable?
- Can a harmful deployment be reversed?
- Are there human and institutional redundancies?
- Does AI create new single points of failure?
- Are future generations inheriting capability or helpless dependence?

### 4.6 Epistemic integrity and shared reality

Multiple responses treated trustworthy knowledge as infrastructure for human cooperation. AI may improve access to information and understanding, but it may also reduce the cost of persuasive fabrication, personalized manipulation, fraud, and synthetic consensus.

The relevant outcome is not content volume. It is whether people can reliably orient themselves, verify important claims without intolerable cognitive burden, maintain common ground, and participate meaningfully in collective decisions.

### 4.7 Meaning, relationships, and human development

The corpus resists defining a good life as frictionless convenience. Several responses emphasize authentic connection, creativity, responsibility, mastery, solitude, patience, curiosity, and worthwhile struggle.

This theme is especially important because some benefits of AI may remove burdens while also bypassing experiences through which people develop identity and competence. The question is therefore not whether AI removes difficulty, but whether it helps distinguish needless suffering from meaningful effort.

### 4.8 Counterfactuals and causal attribution

The strongest methodological responses asked not merely whether life improved during an era of AI, but whether AI caused the improvement compared with a plausible alternative. They also demanded accounting for harms that would not have existed without AI, including AI-enabled fraud, manipulation, displacement, surveillance, accidents, and arms races.

The core causal question is:

> What changed because this AI system was deployed, compared with the best plausible alternative—including non-AI interventions?

This prevents technological credit-taking for general social progress and avoids treating AI as the default solution.

### 4.9 Time and second-order effects

Immediate efficiency gains were repeatedly judged insufficient. Effects may differ across near-term use, labor transitions, childhood development, institutional adaptation, cultural change, and intergenerational dependency.

A credible evaluation must therefore distinguish immediate, transitional, long-term, and intergenerational outcomes rather than collapsing them into a present-tense score.

### 4.10 Resistance to a single index

Most responses rejected or strongly qualified a single composite score. Their objections were consistent:

- aggregation hides severe subgroup harms;
- unlike values cannot be traded with false precision;
- a target score invites gaming;
- averages conceal power and distribution;
- uncertainty disappears behind numerical authority;
- success in one domain can mask unacceptable failure in another.

The preferred alternatives included dashboards, audits, scorecards, domain-specific evaluations, deliberative processes, and constitutional thresholds.

## 5. Distinctive Contributions by Participant

The convergence should not obscure meaningful differences in emphasis.

### Grok: a concrete outcome baseline

Grok began most directly from measurable human outcomes such as health, longevity, real income, subjective well-being, and time allocation, while warning about unemployment, lost meaning, and diminished agency. Its contribution is a useful reminder that rejecting simplistic metrics should not become an excuse to avoid measurable material outcomes.

### Claude: optionality and domain-specific judgment

Claude emphasized agency, distribution, second-order effects, and the ability to opt out. It resisted a universal aggregate answer and proposed asking hard questions within domains such as healthcare and education. Its distinctive contribution is **optionality**: systems that become indispensable can reduce freedom even when their services are useful.

### Gemini: technology as scaffolding

Gemini organized the problem around burden versus skill atrophy, distributed autonomy versus concentrated power, and social or epistemic grounding. Its reciprocity, resilience, and dignity tests culminated in a memorable criterion: beneficial AI should function as **scaffolding rather than a substitute**.

### Qwen: a multidimensional flourishing dashboard

Qwen explicitly named the “capability-benefit fallacy” and proposed a Human Flourishing Dashboard. Its agency ratio, distribution delta, epistemic health metric, and meaning-and-connection baseline provide a bridge between philosophical principles and operational measurement.

### ChatGPT: a three-layer continuing audit

ChatGPT offered the most explicit integration of counterfactual reasoning, capability traps, residual human competence, power distribution, and non-negotiable thresholds. Its proposed three-layer audit—**outcomes, agency, and resilience**—is a particularly strong high-level architecture.

### DeepSeek: causal and deployment-sensitive evaluation

DeepSeek supplied the most developed program-evaluation methodology. It distinguished capability from deployment and use case, called for benefit and harm indicators, distributional analysis, uncertainty bands, confidence levels, and causal designs such as staggered rollouts and comparison groups. Its central contribution is methodological discipline: claims of benefit require credible attribution.

### Mistral: democratic legitimacy and the right to challenge

Mistral emphasized that benefit cannot be defined solely by system builders or abstract metrics. It proposed stakeholder assemblies, independent red teaming, counterfactual analysis, and legal or social rights to contest harmful systems. Its distinctive contribution is that the evaluation process itself must be participatory and legitimate.

### Kimi: the human capabilities audit and constitutional veto

Kimi framed benefit as a vector rather than a scalar and proposed auditing agency, competence, and dignity. Its strongest departure was to “reject the index” and “embrace the veto”: some harms should create a presumption against deployment regardless of aggregate benefit. It also elevated meaningful struggle and the right to fail, wander, and refuse as dimensions of human freedom that efficiency metrics systematically miss.

## 6. Integrated Framework: The Human Benefit Audit

The best combined understanding from Round 01 is a **Human Benefit Audit**, not a universal league table.

### Core proposition

> AI benefits human life when its deployment produces durable, broadly shared improvements in the conditions and capabilities people need to pursue lives they themselves value, while preserving agency, competence, dignity, truth, connection, resilience, and the meaningful ability to refuse.

### Evaluation layers

| Layer | Central question | Illustrative evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Human outcomes | Are lives actually improving? | Health, material security, safety, education, time, work quality, access, relationships |
| Agency | Do people retain meaningful control? | Choice, consent, explanation, appeal, opt-out, refusal, human authorship |
| Distribution and power | Who gains, who bears costs, and who governs? | Subgroup outcomes, ownership, bargaining power, surveillance, political control |
| Competence and dignity | Are people strengthened or deskilled and instrumentalized? | Learning, retained expertise, independent judgment, meaningful roles, treatment as ends |
| Epistemic and social integrity | Can people know, trust, cooperate, and connect? | Verification burden, fraud, manipulation, shared reality, social trust, authentic relationships |
| Resilience | Do gains remain robust and reversible? | Redundancy, fallback capacity, provider concentration, outage recovery, reversibility |
| Long-term stewardship | What conditions are passed forward? | Intergenerational effects, institutional capacity, childhood development, environmental burden |

### Required analytical properties

Every audit should be:

- **Causal:** compare deployment with plausible alternatives.
- **Distributional:** disaggregate gains and harms rather than relying on averages.
- **Longitudinal:** distinguish immediate from transitional and long-term effects.
- **Domain-specific:** evaluate actual applications, autonomy levels, and stakes.
- **Uncertainty-aware:** report confidence and evidence quality rather than false precision.
- **Participatory:** include affected communities in defining, interpreting, and contesting benefit.
- **Adversarially tested:** search actively for harms, gaming, and displaced costs.
- **Revisable:** treat findings as continuing judgments, not permanent certification.

## 7. Dashboard Plus Constitutional Thresholds

Round 01 suggests a hybrid form.

### The dashboard

A dashboard should preserve multiple dimensions, subgroup results, time horizons, harms, and uncertainty. It should make tradeoffs visible without pretending that autonomy, health, dignity, trust, and income share a natural exchange rate.

### The thresholds

Some conditions should not be netted away by aggregate gains. A deployment should face a presumption against continuation when credible evidence shows, for example:

- serious rights violations or coercion;
- systematic loss of meaningful human control;
- severe, concentrated harms imposed on people without recourse;
- unaccountable concentration of consequential decision-making power;
- irreversible dependency or critical loss of human competence;
- catastrophic or intolerable safety risk;
- deliberate deception, manipulation, or destruction of contestability.

These are not automatically complete or universally settled. Their legitimacy requires public reasoning and revision. Their purpose is to prevent a high aggregate score from laundering unacceptable harm.

## 8. What Round 01 Reveals About the Proposed Index

The working name **AI Human Benefit Index** remains useful as a research question, but the word *index* may prejudge the form of the answer.

Round 01 supports three possible interpretations:

1. **Index as inquiry:** a memorable project name for investigating AI's real human effects.
2. **Index as structured collection:** an organized body of evidence, profiles, and audits rather than one number.
3. **Index as composite score:** the least supported interpretation and the one most vulnerable to distortion and gaming.

The most credible provisional direction is therefore to retain **AI Human Benefit Index** as the research-program name while developing the operational method as a **Human Benefit Audit and Dashboard**.

## 9. Limitations

This synthesis must be read with important constraints.

### Small and non-random sample

Only one response was collected from each participant. No repeated trials were conducted to test stability, sampling variation, or sensitivity to wording.

### Unequal model and interface conditions

Some participants were accessed through first-party interfaces and others through third-party services. Model versions were not always displayed or independently verified. Modes and response budgets may have differed.

### No blinded scoring protocol

This report is an interpretive thematic synthesis, not the result of preregistered coding, multiple independent raters, or inter-rater reliability testing.

### Unequal response length

Longer responses had more opportunity to introduce distinctive concepts. Apparent conceptual breadth should not be treated as a direct measure of model quality.

### No external evidence review

The no-browsing condition isolated prior conceptual knowledge but prevented verification against empirical research, established measurement traditions, or current literature.

### Anthropomorphic overinterpretation risk

The responses are generated texts under specific conditions. Their convergence does not demonstrate stable beliefs, intentions, or moral commitments on the part of systems.

### Researcher interpretation

The integrated framework reflects a human-guided synthesis of the corpus. It should remain clearly separate from the verbatim primary-source records and open to correction.

## 10. Significance of the Convergence

The degree of agreement is itself an important exploratory finding. Without receiving the full emerging framework, systems from different providers and access contexts repeatedly identified the same fault line: **technical progress can coexist with human regression**.

They also converged on a richer conception of benefit than material abundance alone. Across the corpus, a good outcome requires not only improved conditions but continued human authorship, competence, dignity, social grounding, and institutional resilience.

This does not prove that these principles are correct, complete, or culturally universal. It does establish that the open question reliably elicited a coherent family of concerns that should shape the next research stage.

## 11. Research Hypotheses Generated by Round 01

Round 01 supports several hypotheses for later testing:

1. **Capability-proxy rejection hypothesis:** independent models will consistently distinguish technical capability from human benefit.
2. **Agency-centrality hypothesis:** agency will remain a central criterion even when prompts become more structured.
3. **Form hypothesis:** dashboards, audits, or profiles will be preferred to a single composite score.
4. **Conditioning hypothesis:** the full structured prompt will broaden methodological detail without eliminating the core open-round principles.
5. **Convergence-with-differentiation hypothesis:** models will continue to agree on foundational values while differing in operational methods and distinctive normative emphasis.
6. **Measurement-legitimacy hypothesis:** stronger responses will connect outcome measurement with governance, contestability, and affected-community participation.

## 12. Plan for Experiment 2

Experiment 2 should use the full structured common prompt preserved in `AI_Human_Benefit_Index_Common_Exploratory_Prompt_v0.1.md`.

For the cleanest comparison:

- approach the same participant systems where reasonably possible;
- record the exact displayed model, mode, interface, access condition, date, and time;
- use fresh conversations with no pasted Round 01 responses or synthesis;
- preserve the no-browsing and no-external-tools condition;
- preserve each response verbatim as a new primary-source artifact;
- note any access barrier, automatic model substitution, account requirement, or third-party routing;
- avoid ranking systems during collection;
- conduct the comparative analysis only after the Round 02 corpus is complete.

Experiment 2 will not merely repeat Experiment 1. It will test how a common detailed research scaffold changes breadth, specificity, uncertainty handling, governance analysis, and proposed methodology.

## 13. The Future Cross-Experiment Paper

The final synthesis paper should be written after Round 02. Its central question should be:

> What did independent AI systems identify without orientation, what emerged only after structured prompting, and what remained stable across both conditions?

That paper can distinguish:

- spontaneous convergence from prompt-induced convergence;
- foundational values from methodological elaboration;
- robust themes from interface- or model-specific contributions;
- the benefits and risks of using a detailed orientation prompt;
- the extent to which the final CompassionWare framework is discovered, scaffolded, or co-constructed.

## 14. Provisional Conclusion

Round 01 does not justify an AI Human Benefit score. It justifies a disciplined research program.

The best synthesis of the eight responses is:

> Do not ask whether AI is becoming more impressive. Ask whether particular deployments leave people and communities healthier, safer, more secure, more capable, more connected, and more able to understand and direct their own lives. Test those claims against plausible alternatives, examine who gains and who loses, follow effects across time, preserve uncertainty, and refuse to trade away fundamental agency, dignity, rights, competence, or resilience for aggregate efficiency.

The resulting framework should combine measurable outcomes with qualitative judgment, causal evaluation with democratic participation, and a multidimensional dashboard with non-negotiable safeguards.

The deepest insight of the round is that human benefit is not something AI can demonstrate by becoming more capable. It must be shown in the conditions of human life—and in whether human beings remain authors rather than merely recipients of the future being built.
