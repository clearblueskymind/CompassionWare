# Common Exploratory Prompt

We are beginning an exploratory research experiment about how the real-world human effects of artificial intelligence might be measured.

AI systems are commonly evaluated for capabilities such as reasoning, coding, planning, tool use, and autonomy. Separate behavioral evaluations may ask how a system handles judgment, uncertainty, power, restraint, or competing values. This inquiry concerns a third and distinct question:

What measurable difference is AI actually making in human lives and society?

The provisional working name is the AI Human Benefit Index (AI-HBI). Neither the name, the idea of an “index,” nor the assumption that a single composite measure is desirable should be treated as settled.

## 1. Concept and Scope

**Challenging the framing.** The working name "AI Human Benefit Index" embeds at least three contested commitments: that the effects in question are *benefits* rather than harms or ambiguous transformations; that they are *human* (as opposed to ecological, interspecies, or systemic); and that an *index*—a composite, typically scalar measure—is the right form. None of these should be taken as given.

"Benefit" presupposes a direction of value that may not be warranted. AI systems often produce gains and losses simultaneously for the same people, or redistribute value in ways that resist simple summation. "Effect" or "consequence" is more neutral and analytically honest. Similarly, "human" arbitrarily brackets ecological and animal impacts that may be inseparable from human wellbeing, though I accept the pragmatic constraint of focusing on human lives if the research community so chooses.

**Is an index the right form?** Almost certainly not, at least not as a primary output. Composite indices (like the Human Development Index) are useful for coarse cross-sectional comparison and political communication, but they are epistemically dangerous when applied to rapidly evolving, causally dense, politically contested phenomena. They compress incommensurable dimensions into a single number, obscure distributional trade-offs, create false precision, and tend to become optimization targets that corrupt the underlying activities (Goodhart's Law). If the goal is understanding rather than ranking, a multidimensional profile or "constellation" is preferable.

**Units of analysis.** No single unit is adequate. I propose a multi-scalar approach:

- **Micro:** Individual interactions with AI systems (a medical diagnosis, a hiring decision, a content recommendation). This captures lived experience but misses structural effects.
- **Meso:** Organizational or sectoral adoption patterns (how a hospital, school, or labor market changes when AI is integrated). This reveals institutional transformation.
- **Macro:** Societal patterns (epistemic environment, political economy, labor market structure). This captures emergent properties invisible at smaller scales.

Each scale requires different methods and yields different answers. The framework should resist collapsing them into one another.

**Knowledge limitation:** I am not deeply familiar with the most recent critiques of composite indices in the specific context of digital technologies post-2023, and my understanding of how national statistical offices are currently handling AI-related measurement is limited to pre-2025 developments.

## 2. Domains and Measures

I propose eight dimensions, selected because they represent distinct spheres of human life that AI is known to affect, and because they are not reducible to one another. The list is provisional and should be contested.

| Dimension | Why Include It | Plausible Outcome Measures (Not Proxies) | Common Proxies to Avoid |
|-----------|----------------|------------------------------------------|------------------------|
| **Bodily health and safety** | AI is deployed in diagnostics, drug discovery, surgery, and physical systems (autonomous vehicles, industrial control). | Changes in morbidity, mortality, medical error rates, occupational injury rates *attributable to* AI-assisted processes. | Number of AI tools approved by regulators; hospital "adoption rates"; investment in health AI. |
| **Economic security and labor conditions** | AI reshapes work, wages, and economic vulnerability. | Changes in real wages, job quality (autonomy, safety, predictability), underemployment, and economic precarity in affected sectors; not just employment counts. | Number of jobs "created" by AI; productivity growth; AI investment volumes; number of workers "reskilled." |
| **Autonomy and agency** | AI can expand or constrain human choice, depending on design and context. | Degree of meaningful human oversight retained in high-stakes decisions; changes in individuals' perceived and actual control over important life decisions. | "Human-in-the-loop" policy statements; explainability scores; user satisfaction ratings. |
| **Social connection and community** | AI mediates and sometimes replaces human interaction. | Changes in loneliness indices, community participation, trust in institutions, and quality of close relationships in contexts where AI is a significant mediator. | Time spent on platforms; "engagement" metrics; number of AI-mediated interactions. |
| **Epistemic environment** | AI shapes what people believe and how they know it. | Changes in misinformation exposure, scientific literacy, trust in evidence-based institutions, and the diversity of information sources actually consumed. | Content moderation volumes; algorithmic "accuracy" scores; number of fact-checks applied. |
| **Political participation and power** | AI affects democratic deliberation, surveillance, and collective self-determination. | Changes in voter suppression, political participation rates, surveillance capacity asymmetries between citizens and states/corporations, and meaningful access to policy influence. | "Digital democracy" initiatives launched; AI used in government services; e-participation platform usage. |
| **Environmental conditions** | AI has significant direct and indirect ecological footprints. | Changes in energy consumption, water use, e-waste, and ecosystem health *causally linked* to AI infrastructure and applications; including rebound effects. | Data center "efficiency" ratings; carbon offset purchases; stated sustainability commitments. |
| **Cultural continuity and diversity** | AI can homogenize or enrich cultural expression. | Changes in linguistic diversity, minority cultural practices, and the viability of non-dominant knowledge systems. | Number of languages supported by models; "cultural content" generated by AI. |

**Critical distinction:** Activity, adoption, capability, investment, and publicity are not outcomes. They are inputs or intermediate variables. A framework that mistakes "more AI in healthcare" for "better health" commits a category error that should be flagged explicitly.

## 3. Attribution and Baselines

**The fundamental problem:** AI never acts alone. An AI-assisted medical diagnosis occurs within a clinical workflow, a regulatory environment, an insurance system, and a pattern of patient behavior. Attributing a change in health outcomes to "the AI" requires isolating its contribution from these co-constitutive factors—a task that is sometimes possible, often approximate, and occasionally meaningless.

**Established methods that may help:**
- **Counterfactual reasoning:** Comparing outcomes in contexts with and without AI exposure, using natural experiments where adoption was quasi-random (e.g., differential rollout across regions or organizations).
- **Synthetic control methods:** Constructing weighted combinations of non-AI-adopting units to estimate what would have happened in adopting units.
- **Contribution analysis:** Abandoning the search for a single causal effect and instead mapping how AI contributed to outcomes alongside other factors, using process tracing and mixed methods.

**Baselines:** The "status quo ante" is often a poor baseline because the counterfactual world without AI is not static—other technologies, policies, and social changes would have occurred. A more defensible baseline is "the most plausible alternative trajectory," though this requires judgment that should be transparent.

**What should remain explicitly unattributed:** Broad societal trends—political polarization, mental health epidemics, changing trust in institutions—where AI is one thread in a dense causal fabric. The framework should have the discipline to say "AI's contribution here cannot be credibly isolated" rather than forcing a number.

**My proposal:** Adopt a "attribution confidence grading" system for each claim, ranging from "well-supported causal estimate" to "plausible contribution" to "unattributable." This prevents the framework from presenting all findings as equally solid.

---

## 4. Uncertainty and Evidence Quality

Uncertainty is not a nuisance to be minimized; it is constitutive of the inquiry. I identify five types:

1. **Statistical uncertainty:** Sampling error, measurement error, variance.
2. **Structural uncertainty:** Uncertainty about model specification and causal structure.
3. **Deep uncertainty (Knightian):** Situations where the underlying probability distributions are unknown or the relevant outcomes are not yet conceivable.
4. **Value uncertainty:** Disagreement about what counts as benefit or harm.
5. **Epistemic uncertainty:** Gaps in available evidence due to under-research (e.g., effects in the Global South, long-term effects).

**How to represent it without false precision:**
- **Confidence grading:** Adapt approaches from evidence-based medicine (e.g., GRADE) to rate the quality of evidence behind each claim.
- **Sensitivity displays:** Show how conclusions change under different assumptions, not just point estimates with confidence intervals.
- **Qualitative uncertainty descriptors:** Use plain-language characterizations ("highly uncertain," "moderately supported," "speculative") alongside any quantitative presentations.
- **Avoid spurious quantification:** Where evidence is qualitative or contested, do not assign numerical scores to create an appearance of rigor.

**My proposal:** The framework should include an "uncertainty dashboard" for each dimension, showing not just the estimated effect but the type and magnitude of uncertainty surrounding it. This is more honest than confidence intervals alone, which can imply a precision that does not exist.

## 5. Distribution and Harm

Aggregate measures are morally and analytically treacherous. A rising average can conceal falling floors, and diffuse benefits do not necessarily offset concentrated harms.

**Who benefits, who bears costs, who is excluded:**
- **Geographic:** Effects likely differ between AI-producing regions (concentrated economic gains, environmental costs) and AI-consuming regions (variable benefits, dependency risks). Urban/rural divides matter.
- **Economic:** Class and wealth determine who gains from productivity enhancements and who bears displacement costs.
- **Demographic:** Age, gender, race, caste, and disability status intersect with AI impacts in ways that aggregate measures erase. For example, facial recognition errors disproportionately affect darker-skinned women; algorithmic management disproportionately affects gig workers.
- **Power:** The most important division may be between those who design, control, and profit from AI and those who are subject to it. This is not reducible to income or geography.

**Harms are not negative benefits.** Some harms are non-compensable. A wrongful arrest based on faulty facial recognition cannot be offset by efficiency gains elsewhere. The framework should maintain a **harms register** separate from benefits accounting, and should flag cases where aggregate gains depend on uncompensated losses.

**Exclusion:** Being left out of AI systems entirely is different from being harmed by them. In some contexts, exclusion is protective; in others, it means missing benefits or being rendered invisible. The framework should track who is excluded and whether that exclusion is voluntary, forced, or structurally imposed.

## 6. Time

**Temporal categories:** I propose distinguishing four horizons, recognizing that boundaries are fuzzy:
- **Immediate (0–2 years):** Direct effects of specific deployments (e.g., a diagnostic tool's accuracy in a pilot hospital).
- **Transitional (2–10 years):** Sectoral restructuring, labor market adjustments, institutional adaptation.
- **Structural (10–30 years):** Changes in skill distributions, power structures, and technological path dependencies.
- **Intergenerational (30+ years):** Effects on children born into AI-saturated environments, cumulative cultural shifts, and irreversible ecological impacts.

**Path dependence and irreversibility:** Early AI deployments can create lock-in effects that constrain future choices. A framework focused only on immediate outcomes misses the option value of keeping alternatives open. Benefits now that foreclose better futures later should be flagged as potentially negative in net present value, depending on ethical commitments.

**Discounting:** Any discount rate embeds a value judgment about the relative worth of present and future lives. Rather than hiding behind a single rate, the framework should present results under multiple discounting assumptions (including zero discounting for certain irreversible harms) and make the ethical choice visible.

**Costs now, benefits later (and vice versa):** AI research and deployment impose current costs (energy, labor exploitation in data annotation, opportunity costs) for uncertain future benefits. The framework should not automatically privilege future promises over present harms.

## 7. Aggregation and Presentation

**I recommend against a single score.** The costs of scalar aggregation outweigh the benefits for this domain. What would be lost:
- **Incommensurability:** Health outcomes and epistemic integrity are not naturally on the same scale. Forcing them onto one requires value judgments that should remain visible and contestable.
- **Distributional information:** A single score can rise while conditions worsen for vulnerable groups.
- **Temporal dynamics:** A score is a snapshot; it cannot represent path dependence or emerging risks.
- **Qualitative texture:** Lived experience of AI-mediated harm or benefit cannot be captured numerically without severe loss.

**Preferred form: A multidimensional "constellation" or profile.** Each dimension is presented separately, with its own scale, uncertainty characterization, and distributional breakdown. Users can see trade-offs explicitly: "Health outcomes improved in wealthy regions; epistemic environment degraded globally; labor precarity increased in specific sectors."

**If synthesis is demanded:** Use structured qualitative judgment rather than mathematical aggregation. A panel of diverse assessors (including affected community representatives) could produce a narrative synthesis that makes disagreements visible. Any weighting scheme should be presented as a scenario, not a discovery.

**What would be lost even in this approach:** The very choice of dimensions privileges certain values over others. The framework cannot be value-neutral, but it can be value-transparent.

---

## 8. The Role of AI in Measurement

**Legitimate roles:**
- **Pattern detection in large unstructured datasets:** Identifying trends in text, image, or behavioral data that would be infeasible for humans to process at scale.
- **Linking disparate data sources:** Integrating information across siloed databases to build more complete pictures of effects.
- **Real-time monitoring:** Tracking rapid changes in domains where human analysis is too slow.
- **Hypothesis generation:** Suggesting unexpected correlations or causal pathways for human investigation.

**Risks requiring safeguards:**
- **Circular self-evaluation:** AI should never be the primary evaluator of AI's own benefits. This creates an obvious conflict of interest and epistemic circularity.
- **Bias amplification:** If training data embeds existing inequalities, AI measurement tools will reproduce and potentially amplify them.
- **Surveillance:** Measurement can slide into monitoring. Gathering detailed data on human behavior to assess AI's effects risks creating new forms of control.
- **Opacity:** If AI systems are used to interpret evidence, their reasoning must be inspectable, not black-boxed.
- **Manipulation:** Evidence gathered for measurement could be used to optimize persuasion or engagement, worsening the very effects being measured.

**Safeguards I propose:**
1. **Mandatory human oversight:** No AI-generated measurement finding enters the framework without human critical review.
2. **Adversarial red-teaming:** Independent teams attempt to corrupt or bias the measurement AI; findings are incorporated into uncertainty assessments.
3. **Transparency labeling:** Every data product must disclose where and how AI was involved in its production.
4. **Data minimization:** Collect only what is necessary for the specific measurement question; resist the temptation to hoard data for future unspecified analysis.
5. **Independence:** Measurement AI should be developed or audited by entities without financial stake in positive findings.

---

## 9. Governance and Resistance to Gaming

**Goodhart's Law and Campbell's Law:** When any measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. If the AI-HBI becomes a corporate ranking, a national competitiveness metric, or an optimization target for AI developers, the incentives to improve the indicator while worsening reality will be overwhelming.

**Governance architecture:**
- **No single owner:** The framework should be stewarded by a distributed, multi-stakeholder body with binding representation from affected communities, civil society, independent researchers, and (in a minority capacity) industry and government.
- **Contestability:** Any actor should be able to challenge specific measurements, methodologies, or the inclusion/exclusion of dimensions. There should be a formal appeals process.
- **Auditing:** Regular independent audits of both the framework and its application in specific cases.
- **Sunset clauses:** Metrics should expire and be rejustified periodically. What made sense in 2025 may not make sense in 2030.
- **Anti-leaderboard design:** The framework should resist being formatted as a ranking. If rankings are demanded by political or market pressures, the stewarding body should refuse or provide them with explicit disclaimers about their limitations.

**Resistance to specific corruptions:**
- **Marketing device:** Prohibit use of the framework in advertising; require independent verification of any public claims.
- **Political instrument:** Do not produce "national AI benefit scores" that could be weaponized in trade or diplomatic contexts.
- **Optimization target:** Explicitly state that the framework is descriptive and evaluative, not normative for design. AI systems should not be trained to maximize scores on this framework.

**Knowledge limitation:** I am not familiar with the most recent institutional innovations in AI governance post-early 2025, and my understanding of how existing composite indices have resisted or succumbed to gaming is based on historical cases (e.g., GDP, university rankings, HDI) rather than AI-specific precedents.

## 10. Research Path and Criticism

**Minimum viable research program:**
Rather than attempting to build a comprehensive index, the MVP should be a **domain-specific pilot program** using mixed methods:

1. **Select 2–3 domains** (e.g., health diagnostics, criminal justice risk assessment, content recommendation) where AI deployment is advanced enough to observe effects but contained enough to study.
2. **Conduct deep case studies** in each, combining quantitative outcome measurement with qualitative ethnographic work to capture lived experience.
3. **Test attribution methods** rigorously, documenting where they succeed and fail.
4. **Develop the uncertainty representation system** through iterative refinement with stakeholders.
5. **Publish "negative results":** When methods fail, document why. This builds epistemic humility into the research culture.
6. **Convene adversarial forums** where critics are resourced to attack the framework's assumptions and methods.

**Existing traditions to draw on:**
- **Development economics:** The capability approach (Sen, Nussbaum) and multidimensional poverty measurement offer frameworks for assessing wellbeing without reducing it to income.
- **Wellbeing economics:** Subjective wellbeing metrics and their critiques provide lessons on the limits of self-reporting.
- **Science and Technology Studies (STS):** Constructive technology assessment and socio-technical systems thinking resist technological determinism.
- **Environmental Impact Assessment:** Long experience with predicting and monitoring complex, long-term, multi-causal effects.
- **Participatory evaluation:** Methods that center affected communities as knowledge producers, not just data sources.
- **Epidemiology:** Causal inference in observational settings with multiple confounders.

**Data and expertise required:** Domain experts (medicine, education, labor), social scientists (sociologists, anthropologists, economists), statisticians, ethicists, legal scholars, community organizers, and affected populations themselves.

**Strongest objections to the project:**
1. **Hubris:** AI's effects are so entangled with other social forces that isolating and measuring them meaningfully may be impossible. The framework may produce sophisticated nonsense.
2. **Capture:** Any framework influential enough to matter will be captured by powerful actors (corporations, states) and turned to their ends.
3. **Legitimation:** The very act of creating a "benefit index" may legitimize AI deployment regardless of findings, providing a veneer of accountability while acceleration continues.
4. **Framing bias:** Starting with "benefit" already tilts the inquiry toward finding positive results; a framework born in this framing may be structurally unable to recognize certain harms.
5. **Opportunity cost:** Resources spent measuring could be spent mitigating known harms.

**Alternative framing/name:** I prefer **"Socio-technical Effects of AI Assessment" (SEAI-A)** or **"AI Human Consequences Constellation"**—names that abandon the index form and the benefit framing in favor of open-ended, multidimensional assessment.

---

## Provisional Recommendation

Do not build an index. Build an **open, contested, multi-scalar assessment framework** that starts with harms and distribution rather than benefits and aggregates. Begin with domain-specific case studies using mixed methods. Make uncertainty, value judgments, and methodological limitations visible rather than hiding them behind numbers. Design the framework to resist being used as a target, a ranking, or a marketing tool. Center the voices of those most affected by AI in defining what matters.

---

## Three Design Principles That Should Not Be Compromised

1. **Incommensurability is a feature, not a bug.** Do not force trade-offs between health, autonomy, epistemic integrity, and ecological stability into a single metric. Keep dimensions separate and let tensions remain visible.

2. **Design for capture.** Assume the framework will be gamed, weaponized, or marketed. Build governance, contestability, and anti-leaderboard features in from the start, rather than adding them as afterthoughts.

3. **Affected communities have standing.** Those who live with AI's consequences—not just those who design, fund, or study it—must have substantive voice in defining what counts as benefit, harm, and appropriate measurement.

---

## Three Genuinely Unresolved Questions

1. **Can any measurement framework avoid becoming an instrument of legitimation?** If creating a framework to measure AI's effects lends credibility to AI deployment regardless of what the framework finds, is the project itself ethically compromised?

2. **How do we represent irreversible, low-probability, high-impact risks within a framework focused on observable human effects?** Catastrophic or existential risks may not show up in standard outcome measures until it is too late to act. Is the framework structurally blind to the most important stakes?

3. **Who has the authority to decide what counts as "human benefit" when different cultures, epistemic traditions, and power positions produce incommensurable answers?** Is a universal framework even coherent, or does it necessarily impose the values of the most powerful measurers on everyone else?
