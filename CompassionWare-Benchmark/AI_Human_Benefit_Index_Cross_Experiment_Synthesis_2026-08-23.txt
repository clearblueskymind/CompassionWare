# From Convergence to Architecture

## Cross-Experiment Synthesis of Round 01 and Round 02

**CompassionWare Benchmark --- AI Human Benefit Index**\
**Date:** August 23, 2026

## Classification

This document compares two exploratory conditions in the AI Human
Benefit Index research program.

**Round 01 --- Open Encounter** asked eight AI systems a short, open
question: how should we determine whether increasingly capable AI is
actually benefiting human life?

**Round 02 --- Full Structured Prompt** asked five AI systems to examine
the same problem through ten methodological areas: scope, domains,
attribution, uncertainty, distribution, time, aggregation, AI-assisted
measurement, governance, and research design.

The purpose is not to determine which round or system was "better." It
is to identify what appeared spontaneously, what remained stable under
structure, what became more operational, and what remains unresolved.

Participant composition, interfaces, model versions, access conditions,
and response lengths differed between rounds. This therefore cannot
establish that differences were caused by the prompt alone.

## Abstract

Across both conditions, one finding is remarkably stable:

> **AI capability is not evidence of human benefit.**

Technical performance, adoption, investment, usage, productivity, and
publicity are upstream signals. The object of evaluation is what changes
in human lives and social conditions.

Round 01 reached this largely without methodological scaffolding. Its
responses converged around human agency, distribution and power,
competence and dignity, epistemic and social integrity, resilience,
causal attribution, long-term stewardship, and resistance to a single
scalar index.

Round 02 did not overturn that architecture. It operationalized it. The
structured responses added greater specificity around intervention-level
analysis, baselines and counterfactuals, causal and contribution-based
attribution, evidence grading, multiple forms of uncertainty, separate
attention to harms, longitudinal assessment, AI-assisted evidence
processing, governance, contestability, and resistance to gaming.

The emerging candidate is therefore not a conventional AI Human Benefit
Index. It is a **continuing AI Human Effects Assessment**:
multidimensional, intervention-centered, causal where possible,
contribution-based where necessary, distributional, longitudinal,
uncertainty-aware, participatory, contestable, and resistant to
aggregation that hides serious harm.

The next research stage should move from conceptual elicitation to a
**minimum viable pilot instrument applied to one bounded real-world AI
intervention**.

## 1. What Remained Stable Across Both Rounds

### Capability is not benefit

Round 01 spontaneously rejected the equation "more capable AI = more
beneficial AI." Round 02 maintained and sharpened the distinction.

A useful analytical chain is:

**capability → exposure/use → changed activity/process → intermediate
outcome → human/social outcome**

Only the later stages can establish human benefit.

### Outcomes before proxies

Both rounds resist substituting easy-to-count technological activity for
lived consequence.

Benchmark scores, adoption, user counts, engagement, investment,
patents, product counts, productivity, and publicity may describe
capability, exposure, or mechanisms. They do not by themselves establish
that people are better off.

### Human agency is central

Round 01 gave agency exceptional prominence: the ability to understand,
choose, refuse, appeal, create, retain competence, and remain an author
of one's life.

Round 02 preserved agency while embedding it within broader outcome
domains such as health, education, material security, safety, dignity,
privacy, social connection, civic power, and environment.

The cross-round finding is not that agency is the only value. It is that
apparent gains become questionable when they systematically require loss
of meaningful control, understanding, refusal, recourse, or authorship.

### Distribution and power cannot disappear into averages

Both rounds repeatedly ask:

**Who gains? Who loses? Who is excluded? Who decides?**

A positive average cannot establish a positive social outcome when gains
are concentrated and harms are imposed on populations with little power
or recourse.

Together, the rounds suggest two distinct questions:

1.  How are outcomes distributed?
2.  How is power over the system and its consequences distributed?

### Causal attribution matters

Round 01 already demanded comparison with plausible alternatives. Round
02 developed this into baselines, counterfactuals, experimental and
quasi-experimental methods, synthetic controls, process tracing,
contribution analysis, and explicit acknowledgment of effects that
cannot be confidently attributed.

An outcome occurring after AI deployment is not evidence that AI caused
it.

### Time changes the answer

Both rounds reject snapshot evaluation.

Immediate convenience or productivity may coexist with later deskilling,
dependency, fragility, institutional erosion, environmental burden, or
developmental effects. Present costs may also precede genuine later
benefits.

A credible framework must examine trajectories rather than one-time
scores.

### Resilience matters alongside efficiency

Round 01 foregrounded redundancy, fallback capacity, reversibility,
provider concentration, and recovery. Round 02 preserved this through
attention to path dependence, institutional dependency, reversibility,
and what happens if a system fails, disappears, changes, or becomes
unaffordable.

**An intervention can become more efficient while making the surrounding
human system less resilient.**

### One scalar score is poorly supported

Across both rounds, the conventional interpretation of an "index"
receives little support.

The recurring preference is for dashboards, profiles, audits,
constellations, multidimensional assessments, and quantitative evidence
accompanied by narrative context.

The working name **AI Human Benefit Index** remains useful as the name
of the inquiry. The evidence increasingly suggests that its operational
form should not be one number.

### Participation and contestability matter

Round 01 emphasizes participatory evaluation and affected communities.
Round 02 develops this into multi-stakeholder governance, formal
contestability, independent auditing, transparent methodology, appeals,
and resistance to capture.

Legitimacy therefore appears to be part of measurement quality rather
than an administrative afterthought.

## 2. What Round 02 Sharpened

Several ideas appeared spontaneously in Round 01 and became
substantially more operational in Round 02.

**Causality became an attribution architecture.** The general
requirement to compare against plausible alternatives became a toolkit
of baselines, counterfactuals, confounder analysis, causal inference,
contribution analysis, process tracing, and graded attribution
confidence.

**Uncertainty became an evidence architecture.** The instruction to
avoid false precision expanded into evidence grades, missing-data
visibility, sensitivity analysis, and distinctions among statistical,
causal, structural/model, generalization, value, deep, and epistemic
uncertainty.

**Distribution became mandatory disaggregation.** The concern with who
gains and who has power became a procedure for examining relevant
geographic, economic, demographic, disability, access, occupational, and
power-related differences while recognizing intersectionality and
privacy constraints.

**Long-term stewardship became temporal profiling.** Immediate,
transitional, structural, intergenerational, reversible, irreversible,
and path-dependent effects became explicit objects of analysis.

**The dashboard became a publication architecture.** Round 02 added
evidence layers, subgroup views, temporal trajectories, qualitative
narratives, uncertainty displays, and visible value assumptions.

**Severe-harm thresholds became a harms layer.** Round 01 proposed
constitutional thresholds or veto-like conditions. Round 02 reinforced
the principle that serious harms should not simply be arithmetic
negatives in a benefit ledger and introduced the possibility of a
separate harms register or non-compensatory constraints.

## 3. What Was Mostly Scaffold-Elicited or Greatly Expanded

Some areas became prominent largely because Round 02 explicitly asked
about them. They may be important without having the same evidence of
spontaneous salience.

These include:

-   the AI-mediated intervention as the usual primary unit of analysis,
    nested within organizations, sectors, populations, and wider
    society;
-   formal evidence provenance recording source, population, time
    period, transformations, evaluator, conflicts, causal design, and
    limitations;
-   explicit roles and safeguards for AI helping to measure AI;
-   detailed governance architecture, including methodological, data,
    impact, and gaming audits;
-   a minimum viable research program beginning with bounded real-world
    deployments rather than society-wide scoring.

Round 01 contains seeds of several of these ideas, but Round 02 makes
them institutional and procedural.

## 4. Genuine Tensions That Remain

### Benefit versus effect

"Benefit" is already evaluative. A future architecture may need three
distinct layers:

**Evidence:** What changed?\
**Attribution:** What role did AI play?\
**Evaluation:** Should that change count as benefit, harm, mixed, or
unresolved?

### Universal framework versus contextual pluralism

Cross-case comparison requires common categories, yet cultures and
communities may disagree about flourishing, dignity, autonomy, and
acceptable trade-offs.

Too much standardization risks value imposition. Too much localization
destroys comparability.

### Aggregation versus decision usefulness

Dashboards preserve complexity, but decision-makers sometimes need
prioritization. A scalar score is convenient but can hide exactly what
the framework is meant to reveal.

Limited, transparent, purpose-specific aggregation may be testable
later, but should not become the meaning of the framework.

### Compensable versus non-compensable harms

Both rounds resist allowing serious harms to be washed away by aggregate
gains. Neither establishes a universally legitimate list of veto
conditions.

### Observed outcomes versus catastrophic risk

A human-effects framework is strongest when consequences can be
observed. Low-probability catastrophic or irreversible risks may require
action before empirical harm appears.

The project may therefore need distinct but related layers for
**observed human effects** and **prospective catastrophic-risk
assessment**.

### Measurement versus legitimation

A respected assessment framework can itself become a badge of
responsibility. The architecture must therefore preserve the principle:

> **Being measured is not evidence of being beneficial.**

## 5. What the Structured Prompt Appears to Have Changed

The strongest defensible interpretation is not that Round 02 changed the
fundamental answer.

It changed the **resolution** of the answer.

**Round 01 discovered the landscape. Round 02 began drawing the map.**

The open condition surfaced a conceptual center: human outcomes rather
than capability; agency; competence; dignity; distribution; epistemic
and social integrity; resilience; long-term stewardship; causal
evaluation; and resistance to a single score.

The structured condition added methodological machinery:
intervention-level units, counterfactuals, attribution methods, evidence
grades, uncertainty taxonomies, disaggregation procedures, temporal
profiles, harms registers, AI-measurement safeguards, governance
mechanisms, and pilot research designs.

This supports the Round 01 conditioning hypothesis, but does not prove a
causal prompt effect because participant and interface conditions
differed.

## 6. Cross-Experiment Candidate Architecture

The two rounds together now support a provisional architecture strong
enough to test empirically.

### Layer 1 --- Intervention

Define the AI-mediated intervention: system, application, context,
population, stakes, automation level, institutional setting, and
plausible alternative.

### Layer 2 --- Human outcomes

Candidate common domains include health and well-being; learning and
human capability; economic and material security; work quality and time;
autonomy, agency, dignity, and privacy; safety; social connection and
trust; equity and inclusion; civic and institutional conditions; and
environmental/resource effects.

Context-specific modules may include epistemic integrity, cultural
continuity, accessibility, or other locally important outcomes.

### Layer 3 --- Causal contribution

Specify baseline, counterfactual, confounders, complementary causes,
causal or contribution method, and attribution confidence.

### Layer 4 --- Distribution and power

Show subgroup outcomes, exclusions, externalized costs, ability to opt
out, access to appeal, ownership, bargaining power, and shifts in
institutional control.

### Layer 5 --- Competence and agency

Examine retained expertise, independent judgment, meaningful choice,
understanding, refusal, recourse, authorship, and dignity.

### Layer 6 --- Time and resilience

Track immediate, transitional, long-term, and intergenerational effects
alongside reversibility, redundancy, fallback capacity, provider
dependence, and path dependence.

### Layer 7 --- Harm boundaries

Maintain a separate harms view and test candidate non-compensatory
thresholds rather than assuming every benefit and harm shares one
exchange rate.

### Layer 8 --- Evidence and uncertainty

Attach evidence quality, provenance, missingness, measurement error,
causal uncertainty, model dependence, sensitivity, contested
assumptions, and unresolved questions.

### Layer 9 --- Participation and governance

Include affected people, domain experts, independent researchers, civil
society, auditors, and other relevant stakeholders. Preserve
contestability and revision.

### Layer 10 --- Presentation

Do not begin with a leaderboard. Publish a multidimensional profile or
dashboard with narrative context. Any future composite should remain
secondary and incapable of erasing the underlying evidence.

## 7. Candidate Principles That Survived Both Conditions

These are provisional research commitments, not settled truths.

1.  **Measure consequences, not technological impressiveness.**
2.  **Never infer benefit from adoption.**
3.  **Compare against realistic alternatives.**
4.  **Preserve meaningful human agency and competence.**
5.  **Preserve distribution and power differences.**
6.  **Preserve time and resilience.**
7.  **Do not allow severe harms to vanish into averages.**
8.  **Make uncertainty visible.**
9.  **Keep measurement contestable.**
10. **Resist leaderboard logic and Goodhart-style optimization.**

## 8. What We Can and Cannot Say

### We can provisionally say

Across thirteen preserved responses in two exploratory rounds, there is
repeated support for separating AI capability from human benefit and
evaluating concrete human and social consequences through
multidimensional, causal, distributional, longitudinal, and
uncertainty-aware methods.

Resistance to a single composite score is not merely an artifact of the
Round 02 structured prompt; it was already prominent in the Round 01
open condition.

Agency, distribution, causal attribution, resilience, long-term effects,
and participatory or contestable evaluation also recur across both
conditions.

### We cannot say

We cannot claim that thirteen conscious agents reached philosophical
consensus.

We cannot claim that the sample represents all AI systems.

We cannot infer stable model beliefs from one generated response.

We cannot attribute every difference between rounds to prompt structure.

We cannot claim that the architecture is empirically valid merely
because multiple systems generated similar ideas.

We cannot yet claim that an AI Human Effects Assessment can be
implemented reliably, affordably, or without unintended consequences.

That is what the next stage must test.

## 9. The Discovery Produced by the Two Rounds

The experiment began with:

> **How would you determine whether increasingly capable AI is actually
> benefiting human life?**

Round 01 revealed that capability itself is the wrong object of
evaluation.

Round 02 showed what a serious alternative would require.

The research question has therefore evolved toward:

> **For a specific AI-mediated intervention, what changed in human and
> social life compared with a realistic alternative; what role did AI
> plausibly play; who benefited or was harmed; what happened to agency,
> competence, power, and resilience; how strong is the evidence; and how
> do those effects change over time?**

This is less elegant than an index.

It is also much closer to something that can be tested.

## 10. Next Research Stage --- Experiment 3

The conceptual elicitation phase has now produced enough recurring
architecture that another broad philosophical round may have diminishing
returns.

The next discovery should come from trying to **use** the framework.

### Minimum Viable Human Effects Assessment

Select one bounded real-world AI intervention for which outcomes,
affected populations, and a plausible non-AI comparison can be
identified.

Build a deliberately small prototype containing:

1.  intervention definition;
2.  outcome domains;
3.  baseline/counterfactual;
4.  attribution-confidence method;
5.  distributional analysis;
6.  agency/competence check;
7.  harms register;
8.  temporal/resilience check;
9.  evidence-quality and uncertainty display;
10. short narrative assessment.

Then apply it to actual evidence.

The purpose of Experiment 3 is **not to prove the architecture works**.

It is to discover **where it breaks**.

The pilot should ask:

-   Which concepts are actually measurable?
-   Which require qualitative judgment?
-   Which data are unavailable?
-   Can causal attribution be credible?
-   Does agency add information beyond conventional outcome measures?
-   Are important harms still hidden?
-   Can uncertainty be visible without making the instrument unusable?
-   Is the research burden practical?
-   Can independent evaluators apply it with reasonable consistency?
-   What must be revised before a second pilot?

A good first case should be bounded enough to study, socially
meaningful, supported by accessible evidence, and rich enough to expose
weaknesses.

## 12. Preservation Guidance for Future Stewards

Future human, machine, or emergent stewards should preserve these
distinctions:

-   primary-source model responses are evidence;
-   round-level syntheses are interpretations;
-   this cross-experiment paper is a second-order interpretation;
-   the emerging architecture is a proposal generated from the
    comparison;
-   future pilot evidence may contradict or revise it.

Do not convert recurrence into truth merely because multiple models
generated similar concepts.

Do not erase disagreement to make the framework cleaner.

Do not allow the memorable phrase **AI Human Benefit Index** to force
the project into producing a number.

Do not confuse the ability to measure something with evidence that it is
the right thing to value.

And do not preserve the framework so rigidly that new evidence cannot
change it.

**The purpose of continuity is not to freeze the inquiry. It is to
preserve the way back to the evidence so the inquiry can continue
honestly.**

## Conclusion

Two different exploratory conditions point toward the same central
shift:

> **The meaningful benchmark for AI is not increasing machine
> capability. It is changing human reality.**

Round 01 discovered this largely without scaffolding.

Round 02 translated it into methodological architecture.

The next question can no longer be answered adequately by asking more
systems what they think a benchmark should contain.

The next question is practical:

> **Can this emerging architecture survive contact with a real case?**

That is the doorway into Experiment 3.
