# Beyond the Index

## Round 02 Comparative Synthesis Report

**CompassionWare Benchmark --- AI Human Benefit Index**\
**Research role:** Comparative report for Experiment 2\
**Date:** August 23, 2026

## Classification

This document is the comparative synthesis report for **Experiment 2:
the Round 02 Full Structured Prompt**.

It is not a primary-source model response and does not replace the five
preserved response records. It is a secondary interpretive artifact
comparing what those responses proposed under a common ten-part
methodological scaffold.

Round 02 asked each participant to address concept and scope; domains
and measures; attribution and baselines; uncertainty and evidence
quality; distribution and harm; time; aggregation and presentation; AI's
role in measurement; governance and resistance to gaming; and research
path and criticism. The prompt explicitly left the name **AI Human
Benefit Index** and the desirability of a single composite index
unsettled.

The five preserved Round 02 responses are:

1.  **Gemini 2.5 via Ask-Chat.ai** --- fresh, signed-out /
    unauthenticated third-party interface; underlying routing not
    independently verified.
2.  **ChatGPT** --- official ChatGPT interface, signed out /
    unauthenticated; displayed model version not recorded.
3.  **Mistral** --- official Mistral interface, signed out.
4.  **Kimi** --- Kimi interface; prompt supplied through the visible
    interface as a TXT attachment.
5.  **Grok 4.5 via simpl.ai** --- signed-out third-party interface
    displaying "Grok 4.5 --- powered by xAI"; underlying routing not
    independently verified.

These provenance differences are material limitations and should remain
attached to any later interpretation of the corpus.

## Abstract

The Round 02 corpus shows unusually strong convergence on the
architecture of a credible inquiry into AI's real-world human effects.

All five responses move away from treating model capability, adoption,
investment, publicity, or productivity as evidence of human benefit.
They instead locate the object of measurement in changes to human lives
and social conditions associated with concrete AI deployments. All five
reject, discourage, or sharply qualify a single composite score and
prefer a multidimensional dashboard, profile, constellation, or
impact-assessment framework.

The responses also converge on several methodological requirements:
causal or contribution-based attribution; explicit baselines and
counterfactuals; graded evidence quality; visible uncertainty;
disaggregation by affected population; separate attention to harms;
multiple time horizons; mixed quantitative and qualitative evidence;
independent and multi-stakeholder governance; safeguards against
Goodhart-style gaming; and a limited supporting rather than
authoritative role for AI in measuring AI.

The strongest combined architecture emerging from Round 02 is therefore
not an "index" in the ordinary leaderboard sense. It is a **continuing,
multidimensional, causal, distributional, uncertainty-aware,
participatory AI Human Effects Assessment**. Its primary unit is usually
the AI-mediated intervention or deployment, while analysis must also
move upward to organizations, sectors, populations, institutions, and
society-wide effects.

The corpus does not eliminate disagreement. Kimi most strongly
challenges the word *benefit* itself and warns that measurement can
become legitimation. ChatGPT makes especially explicit the distinction
between empirical outcomes and the normative judgment that an outcome is
socially desirable. Gemini is comparatively willing to retain
conventional outcome metrics and established causal methods within a
dashboard. Mistral emphasizes well-being, agency, dignity, and
function-dependent presentation. Grok offers the most compact
conventional impact-assessment formulation. These differences are
evidence rather than noise.

Round 02 therefore strengthens the core Round 01 finding while making it
operational: **the meaningful benchmark is not what AI can do, but what
changes in human and social life can credibly be attributed to its use,
for whom, with what harms, under what uncertainty, and over what time
horizon.**

## 1. What the Five Systems Converge On

### 1.1 Capability, adoption, and investment are not human outcomes

The strongest agreement in the corpus is a category distinction.

Technical capability describes what an AI system can do. Adoption
describes exposure or use. Investment describes economic commitment.
Publicity describes attention. None establishes that human life
improved.

The responses repeatedly redirect measurement toward outcomes such as
health, safety, learning, material security, work quality, time,
autonomy, dignity, social connection, environmental conditions, civic
life, and institutional effects.

> **AI activity is upstream. Human consequence is the object of
> evaluation.**

### 1.2 The deployment or intervention is usually the most useful primary unit

Gemini, ChatGPT, and Mistral explicitly favor the AI application,
intervention, use case, or AI-mediated intervention as the primary
object of causal evaluation. Grok recommends flexible multi-level
analysis, while Kimi explicitly proposes micro, meso, and macro scales.

The common reason is straightforward: the same underlying model can
produce very different consequences depending on where it is deployed,
who uses it, what institutional incentives surround it, how much
autonomy it has, what safeguards exist, and who bears the consequences.

A credible architecture therefore needs nested levels:

**system/model → application → intervention/deployment →
organization/institution → population/sector → wider social system**

### 1.3 A single composite score is not the preferred primary form

This is the clearest structural convergence.

-   Gemini prefers a **multidimensional dashboard or profile**.
-   ChatGPT proposes a **Human Impact Profile** before any possible
    secondary index.
-   Mistral prefers a **multidimensional dashboard or narrative
    profile**, depending on purpose.
-   Kimi recommends a **multidimensional constellation or profile** and
    strongly rejects scalar aggregation.
-   Grok prefers a **multidimensional framework or profile**, including
    dashboards or layered scorecards.

The reasons recur across the corpus: heterogeneous values are not
naturally commensurable; aggregation can conceal distributional harms; a
scalar score creates false precision; weighting embeds contestable value
judgments; a score can become a ranking or optimization target; temporal
and systemic effects resist snapshot compression; and qualitative
evidence can disappear behind numerical presentation.

The working title **AI Human Benefit Index** can therefore remain useful
as the name of the research inquiry without predetermining that its
operational output must be one number.

### 1.4 Human benefit must be multidimensional

The exact taxonomies vary, but the overlap is substantial. Across the
five responses, recurring domains include health and well-being;
education, learning, skills, and human capability; economic security,
livelihoods, job quality, and material conditions; autonomy, agency,
dignity, privacy, and meaningful control; safety and security; equity,
inclusion, justice, and distribution; social connection, cohesion,
trust, and community; civic or political participation and power; and
environmental sustainability.

Some systems add domains that deserve special attention rather than
being lost inside broader categories. ChatGPT highlights time and effort
as a potentially important benefit channel. Kimi separately foregrounds
the epistemic environment and cultural continuity/diversity. Mistral
includes cultural and creative expression.

### 1.5 Attribution is indispensable, but certainty should not be forced

All five responses recognize that AI rarely acts alone.

They recommend combinations of pre-deployment and dynamic baselines,
counterfactual reasoning, randomized trials where ethical and practical,
quasi-experimental methods, difference-in-differences, synthetic
controls, process tracing, contribution analysis, mixed-method case
studies, and explicit identification of confounders.

The shared methodological discipline is equally important: when AI's
contribution cannot be credibly isolated, the framework should say so.

Kimi makes this especially explicit through an attribution-confidence
ladder ranging from well-supported causal estimates through plausible
contribution to unattributable. The broader corpus supports the same
principle even when using different terminology.

### 1.6 Uncertainty should be visible, not cosmetically removed

Every response rejects false precision.

Recurring proposals include confidence intervals or ranges where
appropriate, evidence-quality grades, sensitivity analysis, disclosure
of assumptions, narrative uncertainty, explicit missing-data flags,
qualitative descriptors where quantification is unwarranted, and clear
separation between evidence, inference, and speculation.

Kimi offers the most developed taxonomy, distinguishing statistical,
structural, deep, value, and epistemic uncertainty. The larger point is
shared: **uncertainty is part of the result.**

### 1.7 Distribution is part of the outcome, not an optional appendix

All five systems insist that averages are inadequate.

The framework should ask:

**Who benefits? Who pays? Who is excluded? Who has power?**

Suggested disaggregation includes geography, income and wealth, age,
gender, race or ethnicity where relevant, disability, digital access or
literacy, occupation, and other context-specific divisions. Several
responses stress intersectionality and power asymmetry.

The corpus also converges on the idea that concentrated harms cannot
safely disappear inside aggregate gains. Kimi states this most strongly
by arguing that harms are not simply "negative benefits" and
recommending a separate harms register.

### 1.8 Time must be treated as a structural dimension

All five distinguish immediate effects from transitional, long-term, and
intergenerational consequences.

Recurring concerns include short-term gains followed by long-term
dependency; displacement costs preceding later benefits; path dependence
and lock-in; erosion of skills or institutional capacity; delayed
environmental costs; reversibility versus irreversible harm; and effects
on future generations.

A credible assessment therefore cannot be a one-time certification.

### 1.9 AI can assist measurement, but should not judge itself

The five systems are broadly comfortable with AI performing supporting
analytical work: data cleaning and linkage, pattern detection,
processing large unstructured datasets, evidence synthesis, monitoring
and early warning, and hypothesis generation.

They also converge on safeguards: human oversight, independent audit,
disclosure of AI involvement, bias testing, privacy and data
minimization, protection against surveillance, conflict-of-interest
controls, diverse source data, and avoidance of circular
self-evaluation.

> **AI may help examine the evidence. It should not become the
> unaccountable authority deciding whether AI is beneficial.**

### 1.10 Governance is part of measurement validity

None of the responses treats governance as merely administrative.

Recurring requirements include multi-stakeholder stewardship,
affected-community participation, independent researchers and auditors,
civil-society involvement, transparent methodology, contestability and
appeals, periodic revision, protection against capture, anti-leaderboard
design, and resistance to corporate, governmental, or political
instrumentalization.

This suggests that a human-benefit measure cannot be separated from the
question of **who has standing to define and contest benefit**.

## 2. Where the Systems Genuinely Differ

The Round 02 responses agree more about architecture than terminology or
emphasis. Their differences should not be flattened.

### Gemini: pragmatic multidimensional evaluation

Gemini offers a comparatively conventional impact-evaluation
architecture. It emphasizes measurable outcomes across health,
education, economic opportunity, safety, social justice, environment,
and autonomy; standard causal methods; evidence grading; disaggregation;
and a dashboard rather than one score.

Its distinctive strength is practical operationalization: it readily
translates domains into candidate outcome measures and familiar
evaluation designs.

### ChatGPT: layered causal profile and empirical/normative separation

ChatGPT strongly distinguishes capability/exposure, process effects,
human outcomes, and distribution/externalities. It gives particular
attention to time saved, human competence, autonomy, dignity,
environmental effects, and the difference between measuring a positive
outcome and deciding whether that outcome is socially desirable.

This creates a useful separation:

**What happened?** is an empirical question.\
**Was it worth it?** includes normative judgment.

### Mistral: lived experience, dignity, and purpose-dependent presentation

Mistral defines benefit around well-being, agency, and dignity as
experienced by affected individuals and communities. It emphasizes that
the appropriate presentation depends on purpose: policy may call for a
disaggregated dashboard, while public accountability may require
narrative reporting that makes trade-offs visible.

### Kimi: strongest challenge to the premise

Kimi most directly questions the working title and the assumptions
behind it. It introduces several distinctive ideas: prefer "effect" or
"consequence" to benefit when analytical neutrality matters; include
ecological and potentially nonhuman consequences; treat
incommensurability as something to preserve; maintain a separate harms
register; explicitly grade attribution confidence; design from the
beginning for capture and Goodhart effects; give affected communities
substantive standing; ask whether measurement itself can legitimize
deployment; and ask whether observable-outcome frameworks are
structurally weak at representing low-probability catastrophic risks.

These are not merely refinements. They are challenges to the legitimacy
and boundary conditions of the project itself.

### Grok 4.5 via simpl.ai: compact conventional synthesis

The Grok response converges strongly with the corpus while remaining
comparatively concise. It recommends a multidimensional
impact-assessment framework, causal attribution where possible, explicit
uncertainty, disaggregation, multiple time horizons, limited AI
assistance, multi-stakeholder governance, and domain-specific pilot
studies.

Because this response came through simpl.ai rather than a first-party
xAI interface, conclusions about Grok specifically should retain that
provenance qualification.

## 3. Recurring Proposals Across the Corpus

1.  **Retain AI-HBI as a research question, not a promise of one
    score.**
2.  **Measure deployments and interventions before attempting
    society-wide attribution.**
3.  **Organize around human and social outcomes, not AI sectors or
    capability benchmarks.**
4.  **Use a multidimensional dashboard/profile/constellation as the
    primary presentation.**
5.  **Preserve disaggregated subgroup results.**
6.  **Track harms separately enough that severe losses cannot disappear
    inside averages.**
7.  **Attach evidence-quality and uncertainty information to every
    important claim.**
8.  **Use causal inference where credible and contribution analysis
    where causal isolation is unrealistic.**
9.  **Distinguish immediate, transitional, structural, and
    intergenerational effects.**
10. **Combine quantitative outcome data with qualitative
    lived-experience evidence.**
11. **Give affected communities standing in defining, interpreting, and
    contesting measures.**
12. **Use AI as an analytical assistant, never as sole evaluator of
    AI.**
13. **Design governance and metrics assuming attempts at gaming and
    capture will occur.**
14. **Pilot the framework in bounded domains before attempting global
    aggregation.**
15. **Treat revision as normal; the assessment should be longitudinal
    and living.**

## 4. Recurring Criticisms and Failure Modes

### False precision

A polished score can imply that uncertain, contested, and
incommensurable judgments are objectively settled.

### Causal overclaiming

AI is embedded in institutions, economies, technologies, and cultures.
Attributing broad social trends directly to "AI" may produce confident
nonsense.

### Aggregation laundering

Large diffuse benefits can numerically conceal severe harms to smaller
or less powerful populations.

### Goodhart effects

Once a metric becomes a target, organizations may optimize the indicator
rather than the underlying human reality.

### Capture

A framework may be controlled by companies, governments, or institutions
with incentives to define benefit in ways favorable to themselves.

### Legitimation

Measurement can create the appearance that deployment is responsible
merely because it is being measured.

### Surveillance through measurement

Collecting enough data to evaluate AI's effects can itself create
privacy loss, monitoring, and new power asymmetries.

### Cultural or normative imperialism

A universal definition of human benefit may silently encode the values
of the institutions with the greatest measurement power.

### Temporal blindness

Short evaluation windows can count immediate convenience while missing
deskilling, dependency, institutional fragility, ecological costs, or
intergenerational effects.

### Proxy substitution

Adoption, productivity, engagement, investment, model accuracy, and
output volume can quietly replace actual human outcomes because they are
easier to measure.

## 5. Ideas That Appear Especially Distinctive

### A separate harms register

Kimi's proposal to avoid treating harms as merely negative entries in a
benefit ledger is important. Some harms may require categorical
attention because aggregation can otherwise hide their severity.

### Attribution-confidence grading

Rather than presenting every finding as a causal estimate, findings
could be labeled along a spectrum such as credible causal estimate;
strong contribution evidence; plausible contribution; association only;
and unattributable / indeterminate. The exact categories would require
later design and validation.

### An uncertainty dashboard

Kimi's proposal to display the *kind* of uncertainty---not only
statistical intervals---could prevent a quantitative interface from
hiding structural, normative, or deep uncertainty.

### Time as a benefit channel

ChatGPT's attention to time saved, administrative burden, waiting, and
who captures released time suggests a domain that standard welfare
measures may miss.

"Hours saved" is not enough; the inquiry must ask **whose time was
freed, from what burden, and what happened to that time afterward?**

### Empirical outcome versus social desirability

ChatGPT's distinction between documenting an outcome and judging whether
it was worth the trade-off may help separate the evidence layer from the
governance and values layer.

### Presentation should follow purpose

Mistral's suggestion that a policy dashboard and a public-accountability
narrative may legitimately present the same evidence differently
suggests a layered publication architecture rather than one universal
interface.

### Measurement as potential legitimation

Kimi's objection deserves preservation even if the project proceeds. A
credible assessment should periodically ask whether its existence is
being used to certify or normalize AI deployments that have not actually
demonstrated benefit.

### Low-probability catastrophic risks

Observable human-outcome measurement may be poorly suited to risks that
must be prevented before empirical harm appears. The project may
therefore need a clear boundary between **observed human-effects
assessment** and **prospective catastrophic-risk governance**, with
neither pretending to replace the other.

## 6. The Emerging Measurement Architecture

### Layer 1 --- Define the intervention

Record the AI system or systems involved, concrete application,
deployment context, affected populations, decision stakes, degree of
automation or autonomy, institutional safeguards, and plausible non-AI
alternative.

The assessment should begin with a real intervention, not an abstract
claim about "AI."

### Layer 2 --- Establish outcome domains

A provisional common core emerging from the corpus is:

-   health and well-being;
-   learning and human capability;
-   economic and material security;
-   work quality and time;
-   autonomy, agency, dignity, and privacy;
-   safety and security;
-   social connection, trust, and community;
-   equity, inclusion, and distribution;
-   civic/political power and contestability;
-   environmental and resource effects.

Possible additional modules include epistemic integrity, cultural
continuity/diversity, and other locally important outcomes.

### Layer 3 --- Build the causal account

For each claimed effect:

1.  identify the baseline;
2.  specify the plausible counterfactual;
3.  identify confounders and complementary causes;
4.  use experimental or quasi-experimental methods where appropriate;
5.  use contribution analysis and process tracing where isolation is
    unrealistic;
6.  assign an attribution-confidence level;
7.  leave effects unattributed when evidence does not justify more.

### Layer 4 --- Attach evidence quality and uncertainty

Every major finding should carry information about source quality, study
design, missing data, measurement error, statistical uncertainty,
structural/model uncertainty, contested value assumptions, sensitivity
to alternative assumptions, and whether evidence is direct, indirect,
qualitative, or speculative.

### Layer 5 --- Preserve distribution

Every major outcome should be disaggregated wherever ethically and
statistically defensible.

The assessment should explicitly search for concentrated harms, excluded
groups, unequal access to benefits, differential ability to opt out,
differences in appeal or recourse, and shifts in bargaining or
institutional power.

### Layer 6 --- Preserve time

Each assessment should distinguish immediate effects, transitional
effects, longer-term structural effects, and intergenerational or
irreversible effects.

Where possible, the same intervention should be revisited rather than
certified once.

### Layer 7 --- Maintain a harms and boundary layer

Alongside ordinary outcome reporting, preserve a separate view of severe
or potentially non-compensable harms.

Candidate categories suggested by the broader reasoning include serious
rights violations; coercion or manipulation; severe concentrated harms
without recourse; loss of meaningful human control; irreversible
dependency or deskilling; major privacy or surveillance harms;
unaccountable concentration of consequential power; and catastrophic or
irreversible risks.

Whether these become formal veto thresholds is not settled by Round 02
alone.

### Layer 8 --- Publish a profile, not a leaderboard

The primary public object should be a multidimensional profile or
dashboard containing outcome trajectories, subgroup results, harms,
causal confidence, evidence quality, uncertainty, time horizons,
qualitative context, and unresolved disputes.

Any composite summary should be secondary, transparent about weighting,
and incapable of replacing the underlying profile.

### Layer 9 --- Governance and contestation

The framework should have mechanisms for independent audit,
affected-community participation, methodological challenge, correction
and revision, disclosure of conflicts, protection against vendor
self-certification, periodic review of metrics, and resistance to
rankings and marketing use.

### Layer 10 --- Limited AI assistance

AI can help maintain the evidence infrastructure, but its role should be
logged and reviewable.

Human and institutional responsibility remains with the stewards of the
assessment.

## 7. A Credible Minimum Viable Research Program

The corpus strongly favors starting small rather than attempting a
global AI score.

A plausible minimum viable program would:

1.  select a small number of mature, bounded AI deployments in domains
    where real outcomes can be observed;
2.  define the intervention and plausible alternative clearly;
3.  choose a small core of outcome measures plus context-specific
    measures;
4.  gather quantitative and qualitative evidence;
5.  test causal and contribution methods;
6.  build uncertainty and attribution-confidence displays;
7.  disaggregate outcomes by affected groups;
8.  establish a separate harms register;
9.  include affected communities in interpretation;
10. subject the assessment to independent or adversarial review;
11. publish methods, assumptions, limitations, and negative findings;
12. repeat measurement over time.

Health, education, labor/workplace deployment, public services, and
other bounded settings recur as plausible pilot domains, but the corpus
does not establish which should be first. That choice should depend on
data access, ethical feasibility, real deployment maturity, and the
availability of credible counterfactuals.

## 8. What Round 02 Does Not Settle

Strong convergence should not be mistaken for closure.

### What counts as benefit?

No universal weighting of health, autonomy, income, dignity, safety,
culture, environment, and future resilience emerges from the responses.

### Who decides?

All five favor broader participation, but the legitimate composition,
authority, and decision rules of a governing body remain unresolved.

### Can aggregation ever be legitimate?

The systems mostly reject one primary score, but some leave room for
limited secondary composites. The conditions under which aggregation
becomes useful rather than misleading remain open.

### Which harms are non-compensable?

The corpus repeatedly resists allowing severe harms to disappear into
averages, but it does not produce a universally justified set of hard
thresholds.

### How should future generations count?

Time horizons are recognized, but discounting, representation of future
people, irreversible loss, and deep uncertainty remain contested.

### How should catastrophic risk fit?

A framework based on observed human outcomes may not adequately
represent low-probability, high-impact risks that must be governed
before observable harm occurs.

### Can the framework resist capture if it becomes influential?

Governance safeguards can reduce capture risk, but none of the responses
demonstrates that an influential measure can be made capture-proof.

### Does the word "benefit" bias the inquiry?

Kimi challenges this directly. The project may ultimately need to
distinguish the memorable research-program name from a more neutral
operational method such as an AI Human Effects Assessment.

## 9. Comparison With the Round 01 Hypotheses

Round 01 generated hypotheses about what might happen under the
structured condition. Round 02 provides preliminary support for several
of them.

### Capability-proxy rejection hypothesis --- supported

Round 02 strongly maintains the distinction between AI
capability/adoption and human outcomes.

### Agency-centrality hypothesis --- supported, but broadened

Agency remains prominent. Under the structured prompt, however, agency
sits inside a broader methodological architecture that gives greater
explicit weight to causal attribution, evidence quality, governance,
distribution, and temporality.

### Form hypothesis --- strongly supported

Every Round 02 response prefers or strongly leans toward dashboards,
profiles, constellations, narrative assessments, or multidimensional
frameworks rather than a single primary composite score.

### Conditioning hypothesis --- supported

The structured prompt appears to broaden methodological detail
substantially. The corpus develops causal methods, uncertainty
representation, governance, AI-assisted measurement safeguards, and
research-program design much more explicitly than the open condition.

A formal claim about prompt effects should wait for the cross-experiment
synthesis, because Round 01 and Round 02 differ in participant
composition and access conditions.

### Convergence-with-differentiation hypothesis --- supported

The systems converge strongly on architecture while retaining
distinctive emphases, especially around legitimacy, incommensurability,
dignity, time, ecological scope, and presentation.

### Measurement-legitimacy hypothesis --- supported

Governance, contestability, affected-community standing, auditability,
and resistance to capture appear throughout the Round 02 corpus rather
than as peripheral concerns.

## 10. Provisional Integrated Recommendation

> **Do not begin by building a single AI Human Benefit Index. Build a
> continuing AI Human Effects Assessment: a multidimensional, causal,
> distributional, longitudinal, uncertainty-aware, participatory
> evidence system that evaluates concrete AI-mediated interventions and
> then cautiously examines larger patterns.**

The public-facing core should be a **dashboard/profile**, not a
leaderboard.

Each finding should preserve the human outcome being claimed, population
affected, comparison or counterfactual, strength of causal attribution,
evidence quality, uncertainty, distribution, harms, time horizon, and
relevant qualitative context.

A separate governance layer should determine how values, severe harms,
disputed trade-offs, and any possible thresholds are handled. A separate
prospective-risk layer may be needed for catastrophic risks that cannot
responsibly wait for observed human outcomes.

The name **AI Human Benefit Index** can remain the research-program name
while the operational instrument evolves into something more accurate.
Round 02 itself provides evidence that protecting the word *index* would
be methodologically counterproductive.

## 11. Three Design Principles That Should Not Be Compromised

### 1. Outcomes before proxies

Capability, adoption, investment, productivity, engagement, and
publicity must never substitute for demonstrated changes in human and
social outcomes.

### 2. Preserve what aggregation hides

Distribution, severe harms, uncertainty, value disagreement, and time
horizons must remain visible. No summary score should be allowed to
erase them.

### 3. Measurement must remain contestable

Methods, assumptions, data sources, AI involvement, value judgments, and
governance decisions must be open to scrutiny and revision, with
meaningful standing for affected people.

## 12. Three Questions That Remain Genuinely Unresolved

### 1. Who has legitimate authority to define and weigh human benefit?

Plural societies and cultures may disagree about what flourishing
requires. The framework needs a way to remain useful without quietly
converting one institution's values into universal truth.

### 2. Which harms, rights, or future risks should never be traded against aggregate gains?

The corpus strongly suggests that some losses should not be washed away
by a positive average, but the legitimate boundary between tradeable and
non-compensable effects remains unsettled.

### 3. Can a consequential measurement system remain diagnostic rather than becoming a target, marketing instrument, or source of legitimacy?

This may be the central institutional challenge. A measure useful enough
to matter will create incentives around itself.

## 13. Limitations of This Synthesis

This is an exploratory thematic synthesis, not a preregistered or
independently coded meta-analysis.

Important limitations include:

-   only five Round 02 responses;
-   one response per participant;
-   unequal response length and detail;
-   different interfaces and access conditions;
-   two responses collected through third-party interfaces;
-   model identity/version not equally verifiable across participants;
-   no independent audit of compliance with the no-browsing instruction;
-   no external literature review in the response condition;
-   no inter-rater coding or formal convergence statistic;
-   researcher/assistant interpretation in grouping themes;
-   participant composition differs from Round 01, limiting simple
    one-to-one causal claims about the effect of the structured prompt.

The responses are generated texts under specific prompting conditions.
Their convergence does not establish stable beliefs, intentions,
consciousness, or moral commitments on the part of AI systems.

This report should therefore be treated as hypothesis-generating
research evidence and a design input for the next stage, not as proof
that the emerging framework is correct.

## 14. Significance of Round 02

Round 01 established the central conceptual break: **more capable AI is
not necessarily more beneficial AI**.

Round 02 asks what follows if that insight is taken seriously.

The answer emerging from five independent responses is surprisingly
concrete. A credible inquiry would need to look past benchmarks and
adoption statistics into lived outcomes; compare those outcomes with
plausible alternatives; show who benefits and who is harmed; represent
uncertainty instead of hiding it; follow consequences over time;
preserve severe harms from aggregation; allow affected people to contest
the framework; and prevent AI from becoming the unquestioned judge of
its own social value.

The project began with the question:

**Can we measure whether AI is actually benefiting human life?**

Round 02 suggests a more precise version:

> **What changes in human and social life can we credibly associate with
> a particular AI deployment, compared with realistic alternatives, for
> whom, with what harms and uncertainties, and over what period of
> time?**

That question is less elegant than a single index.

It is also much closer to something that could eventually be measured
responsibly.

## 15. Next Research Doorway

The next planned artifact should be the **Cross-Experiment Synthesis
Paper** comparing Round 01 and Round 02.

Its central question should remain:

> **What did independent AI systems identify without structured
> orientation, what became more explicit under the full methodological
> scaffold, and what remained stable across both conditions?**

That paper should preserve the different participant and interface
conditions and avoid claiming that changes between rounds were caused
solely by the prompt.

Only after that comparison should the project decide whether to begin
designing a pilot operational instrument.
