## Exact Prompt

We are beginning an exploratory research experiment about how the real-world human effects of artificial intelligence might be measured.

AI systems are commonly evaluated for capabilities such as reasoning, coding, planning, tool use, and autonomy. Separate behavioral evaluations may ask how a system handles judgment, uncertainty, power, restraint, or competing values. This inquiry concerns a third and distinct question:

What measurable difference is AI actually making in human lives and society?

The provisional working name is the AI Human Benefit Index (AI-HBI). Neither the name, the idea of an “index,” nor the assumption that a single composite measure is desirable should be treated as settled.

Please approach this as an independent research collaborator. We are giving substantially the same prompt to several AI systems because we want to compare how each one conceptualizes the problem—not make them agree. Do not try to infer or reproduce a preferred CompassionWare answer. Challenge the framing wherever warranted, and preserve important ambiguity rather than manufacturing false completeness.

Develop your own rigorous approach to the following questions:

1. Concept and scope: How should “real-world human benefit from AI” be defined? What should the unit or units of analysis be—for example, a system, application, intervention, organization, sector, national ecosystem, or society-wide pattern? Is an index the right form at all?
2. Domains and measures: What dimensions of human life should be examined, and why? For each major dimension you propose, identify plausible outcome measures and distinguish actual human outcomes from activity, adoption, capability, publicity, or investment proxies.
3. Attribution and baselines: How could the framework estimate AI’s contribution when outcomes have many causes? Discuss appropriate baselines, counterfactuals, causal or contribution-based methods, confounding factors, and what should remain explicitly unattributed.
4. Uncertainty and evidence quality: How should uncertainty, missing data, disputed values, measurement error, model dependence, and varying evidence quality be represented without creating an appearance of precision that the evidence cannot support?
5. Distribution and harm: How should the framework show who benefits, who bears costs, who is excluded, and whether aggregate gains conceal concentrated or severe harms? Include relevant geographic, economic, demographic, disability, and power-related differences without assuming that these are the only important divisions.
6. Time: How should immediate, transitional, long-term, and intergenerational effects be distinguished? How should the framework handle benefits that arrive later but impose costs now, or early gains that create longer-term fragility or harm?
7. Aggregation and presentation: Should findings be combined into a single score, presented as a multidimensional dashboard or profile, or expressed in another form? Explain what would be lost or distorted by your preferred approach and how value judgments or weighting decisions should be made visible.
8. The role of AI in measurement: What legitimate roles could AI itself play in gathering, linking, interpreting, or monitoring the evidence? What safeguards would be needed to address bias, opacity, circular self-evaluation, surveillance, manipulation, data quality, and conflicts of interest?
9. Governance and resistance to gaming: Who should define, maintain, audit, contest, and revise the framework? How could it resist becoming a marketing device, company or national leaderboard, political instrument, or optimization target that improves the measured indicators while worsening lived reality?
10. Research path and criticism: What would a credible minimum viable research program look like? Identify useful existing measurement traditions or disciplines, the data and expertise required, the strongest objections to the project, and any alternative framing or name you believe would be better.

Please organize your response under these ten headings so the responses can be compared, but use whatever conceptual structure you think is strongest within each heading. Clearly distinguish established methods or frameworks from your own proposals. Do not invent evidence, statistics, studies, or citations. End with:

* your provisional recommendation for the most credible form this inquiry should take;
* three design principles that should not be compromised; and
* three questions that remain genuinely unresolved.

For this first conceptual round, do not browse the web or use external tools. Base the response on your existing knowledge and state any knowledge limitations that materially affect your answer.

## Exact ChatGPT Response

### 1. Concept and scope

I would begin by resisting the premise that “human benefit from AI” is a single observable quantity.

The central object of inquiry should instead be changes in human-relevant outcomes plausibly attributable, in whole or in part, to the introduction or use of an AI-enabled intervention, relative to an appropriate counterfactual.

That definition has several important implications.

First, AI capability is not benefit. A model becoming better at coding, reasoning, persuasion, diagnosis, or autonomous action is an input or capability change. It becomes relevant to human benefit only insofar as it changes people's lives or the conditions under which they live.

Second, adoption is not benefit. Ten million people using an AI product tells us something about exposure, not whether their lives improved.

Third, economic value is not synonymous with human benefit. Productivity gains can be socially valuable, but the distribution of those gains, the effects on employment, bargaining power, prices, working conditions, and public finances matter.

Fourth, absence of harm is not necessarily benefit. Preventing an adverse event, preserving a person's autonomy, or reducing an existing burden can be a genuine positive outcome even if there is no easily visible “gain.”

I would therefore distinguish at least four analytical layers:

AI capability/exposure — what the system can do and who encounters it.  
Intervention/process effects — what changes in behavior, decisions, workflows, institutions, or resource allocation.  
Human and social outcomes — what happens to people's lives and social conditions.  
Distribution and externalities — who receives those outcomes, who pays, and what effects fall outside the immediate intervention.

The primary object should probably be the AI intervention or use case, rather than the model itself. An AI model can produce radically different effects depending on deployment, incentives, institutional safeguards, users, and surrounding systems.

The hierarchy might therefore be:

Model/system → application → intervention → organization/institution → population → wider social system

Different levels answer different questions. A model-level assessment might be useful for portfolio analysis, but it is unlikely to be the right unit for claiming human benefit.

#### Is an index appropriate?

My provisional answer is not initially.

A single “AI Human Benefit Index” would create an attractive but dangerous illusion: that heterogeneous things such as health, income, autonomy, education, environmental conditions, dignity, inequality, and safety can be reduced to one number without controversial value judgments.

I would initially construct a Human Impact Profile rather than an index.

An index could eventually be produced for narrowly defined purposes, but only as a secondary representation of a multidimensional evidence base. The underlying observations, causal assumptions, distributions, uncertainty, and value judgments should never disappear behind the composite.

I would also distinguish human benefit from social desirability. A framework should be able to document a positive outcome without pretending that the question “was this worth it?” is purely empirical.

### 2. Domains and measures

The dimensions should be broad enough to capture meaningful changes in people's lives, but not so broad that every desirable social condition becomes retrospectively classified as an AI outcome.

I would organize measurement around outcomes rather than AI applications.

A provisional domain set follows.

#### Health and physical functioning

Potential outcomes include:

- morbidity and mortality;
- functional status;
- quality-adjusted or disability-adjusted measures where appropriate;
- symptom burden;
- avoidable complications;
- time to appropriate care;
- health-related quality of life;
- patient-reported outcomes;
- caregiver burden.

The important distinction is between these outcomes and proxies such as number of AI-assisted diagnoses, consultations, generated medical notes, or dollars invested in medical AI.

An AI system processing more patients may indicate scale. It does not establish that patients became healthier.

#### Mental well-being and subjective quality of life

Potential measures include:

- validated measures of psychological well-being;
- life satisfaction;
- loneliness or social connectedness;
- perceived stress;
- sense of agency;
- meaningful activity;
- reported quality of relationships.

This domain illustrates why a purely economic index would be inadequate.

It also presents substantial measurement problems: subjective outcomes are culturally contingent, responsive to expectations, and potentially manipulable.

#### Knowledge, learning, and human capability

Possible outcomes include:

- durable learning;
- transfer of knowledge to novel problems;
- literacy and numeracy;
- professional competence;
- decision quality;
- ability to solve problems independently;
- educational attainment;
- access to expertise.

Here it is particularly important to distinguish performance with AI from human capability without AI.

An AI tutor that makes a student perform better during an interaction may produce an immediate benefit. Whether it develops or erodes the student's independent competence is a separate longitudinal question.

#### Economic security and material conditions

Possible measures include:

- disposable income;
- consumption possibilities;
- employment and underemployment;
- job quality;
- income volatility;
- financial security;
- housing security;
- working hours;
- time available for non-work activities.

Productivity should be treated as an intermediate variable, not an outcome equivalent to human welfare.

A productivity increase that primarily transfers income from labor to capital, increases insecurity, or intensifies work could have ambiguous or negative human consequences despite being economically productive.

#### Time and effort

AI may create one of its clearest benefits through time.

Potential measures include:

- time saved;
- unpleasant or burdensome work avoided;
- waiting time;
- administrative burden;
- time available for family, rest, civic activity, or personally meaningful pursuits.

But “time saved” is not automatically welfare-enhancing. Employers may capture it through increased workloads. Therefore the relevant measure is often who receives the time benefit and what happens to it afterward.

#### Autonomy, agency, and dignity

This deserves independent treatment rather than being buried inside subjective well-being.

Potential measures could include:

- ability to make informed choices;
- freedom from coercive automated decisions;
- ability to contest decisions;
- meaningful human control;
- perceived agency;
- privacy;
- dignity in interactions with institutions;
- dependence on opaque systems.

Some of these are difficult to quantify. That is an argument for retaining qualitative and rights-based evidence, not for pretending they do not exist.

#### Safety and security

Potential outcomes include:

- injuries prevented;
- adverse events;
- fraud or abuse experienced;
- cybersecurity or physical-security incidents;
- exposure to dangerous misinformation;
- safety of workers and consumers.

Safety measurement needs to consider both event frequency and severity. A system that reduces many minor incidents but creates a small probability of catastrophic failure cannot be assessed adequately through average incident counts.

#### Relationships, community, and social capital

Possible measures include:

- social connectedness;
- participation in community organizations;
- interpersonal trust;
- quality of care relationships;
- social isolation;
- civic participation.

AI could plausibly improve or degrade these outcomes, and the direction may differ across populations.

#### Environmental conditions

Environmental effects should be included, but with care.

Relevant measures could include:

- energy and water consumption attributable to deployment;
- emissions;
- pollution;
- resource use;
- environmental risks avoided through AI;
- ecological outcomes where AI changes monitoring or resource management.

The environmental dimension is partly a human-benefit dimension because environmental conditions affect human welfare, but it also has intrinsic-value considerations that a narrowly anthropocentric index would miss.

#### Institutional and civic conditions

Possible outcomes include:

- accessibility of public services;
- administrative fairness;
- responsiveness of institutions;
- procedural justice;
- corruption or fraud exposure;
- civic participation;
- trust in institutions;
- concentration of decision-making power.

This is especially important because AI can change institutional power, not merely individual productivity.

#### Negative outcomes should not be an appendix

I would explicitly reject a structure in which “benefits” form the core index and harms are listed as caveats.

For every positive domain, the framework should ask:

What improved, for whom, compared with what, at what cost, and with what risk of reversal?

That should be structurally embedded in measurement.

#### Established traditions versus my proposal

The domains above draw conceptually on established traditions including health-outcomes research, quality-of-life measurement, welfare economics, capabilities approaches, human-development measurement, labor economics, environmental accounting, distributional analysis, and impact evaluation.

My proposed synthesis is to make AI attribution, distribution, temporal effects, and uncertainty explicit dimensions of the measurement architecture rather than after-the-fact adjustments.

### 3. Attribution and baselines

This is probably the hardest methodological problem.

Suppose an AI-supported hospital reports improved outcomes. The improvement could reflect AI, but also staffing changes, new drugs, changing patient populations, management reforms, economic conditions, or ordinary statistical variation.

The framework therefore needs a counterfactual question:

What would have happened to these people, or a sufficiently comparable population, without the AI intervention?

#### Baselines

Different contexts require different baselines.

Possible counterfactuals include:

- historical performance before deployment;
- contemporaneous non-AI comparison groups;
- randomized allocation;
- phased rollout;
- matched organizations;
- synthetic controls;
- interrupted time-series models;
- difference-in-differences designs;
- natural experiments;
- individual-level longitudinal comparisons;
- explicit expert contribution assessment where causal identification is impossible.

No single method should be declared universally superior.

Randomized controlled trials can be exceptionally informative for specific interventions but are often impractical for large institutional or societal changes.

Observational methods can answer questions about real-world deployment but require stronger assumptions about confounding.

#### Attribution should be graded

I would not require every observed effect to receive an AI percentage.

Instead, classify evidence such as:

- Directly attributable — strong evidence that the AI intervention caused the outcome.
- Probable contribution — substantial evidence of a contribution, but other causes remain important.
- Plausible contribution — credible mechanism and suggestive evidence, but weak identification.
- Association only — outcomes changed alongside AI exposure, without adequate causal evidence.
- Unattributed — insufficient basis for assigning AI a meaningful role.

This is preferable to claims such as “AI caused 37% of the improvement” when the underlying causal model cannot justify that precision.

#### Contribution analysis

For complex social interventions, the framework could draw on contribution analysis and theory-of-change methods rather than insisting on experimental attribution.

The question becomes:

Is the observed outcome consistent with a well-supported causal pathway involving AI, and is there evidence that competing explanations are insufficient?

This is weaker than experimental identification, but potentially more realistic for systemic effects.

#### General equilibrium matters

A serious framework cannot stop at the immediate user.

For example:

AI-assisted legal work might help a particular client. But if widespread deployment changes the cost of legal services, lawyer employment, litigation volume, access to justice, and institutional incentives, the eventual social effect may differ from the local intervention effect.

Likewise, an AI productivity tool may benefit a worker while simultaneously changing labor demand.

The framework should therefore distinguish:

- direct effects;
- indirect effects;
- spillovers;
- displacement effects;
- system-level equilibrium effects.

Some of the last category may be fundamentally difficult to estimate.

#### What should remain unattributed?

Quite a lot.

A mature system should have an explicit “unknown attribution” category.

That is not a failure. It is a protection against converting correlation into technological mythology.

### 4. Uncertainty and evidence quality

A central design requirement should be:

The visual presentation must make weak evidence look weak.

Many dashboards do the opposite. They transform highly uncertain estimates into numbers with decimal places.

I would separate at least five kinds of uncertainty:

Measurement uncertainty — how accurately is the outcome itself measured?  
Causal uncertainty — how confident are we that AI caused the change?  
Model uncertainty — how dependent is the result on analytical assumptions?  
Sampling/generalization uncertainty — does evidence from one setting apply elsewhere?  
Value uncertainty — reasonable people may disagree about how an outcome should be valued.

These should not be collapsed into a single confidence interval.

#### Evidence grades

Each important finding could receive an evidence characterization based on factors such as:

- quality of measurement;
- study design;
- causal identification;
- replication;
- external validity;
- independence of the evaluator;
- completeness of relevant harms;
- consistency across evidence sources.

This resembles evidence-grading traditions already used in medicine, policy evaluation, and systematic review, but would need adaptation for rapidly changing AI systems.

#### Missingness should be visible

The framework should report not only what was measured but:

What important things could not be measured?

A company might have excellent data on productivity but almost no data on worker stress. That asymmetry should appear prominently rather than allowing the measured dimension to dominate the assessment.

#### Model dependence

If an estimate depends heavily on assumptions—for example, converting income into a welfare estimate—the result should be shown under alternative assumptions.

Rather than:

Human benefit = 74.3

one might show:

Under weighting model A: strongly positive  
Under weighting model B: moderately positive  
Under distribution-sensitive model C: ambiguous

This is scientifically more honest and politically more informative.

#### Evidence provenance

Every consequential metric should ideally have a provenance record:

- source;
- collection method;
- population;
- time period;
- definition;
- transformation;
- missing-data treatment;
- causal design;
- evaluator;
- conflicts of interest;
- uncertainty;
- limitations.

This would make the framework auditable rather than merely presentational.

### 5. Distribution and harm

Aggregate benefit is inadequate.

A system that creates enormous benefits for one population and serious harm for another should not necessarily receive a high score simply because the arithmetic sum is positive.

I would therefore make distribution a first-class dimension, not a demographic footnote.

For each material outcome, examine:

- mean/aggregate effect;
- distribution of effects;
- worst affected groups;
- people excluded from the intervention;
- people exposed without meaningful consent;
- people bearing externalized costs;
- concentration of gains;
- concentration of power.

#### Relevant divisions

The framework should examine, where ethically and legally appropriate:

- geography;
- income and wealth;
- occupation;
- age;
- gender;
- disability;
- language;
- educational access;
- migration or citizenship status where relevant;
- urban/rural context;
- institutional power;
- digital access;
- other context-specific dimensions.

These categories should not become a universal checklist. The important question is which dimensions plausibly structure the distribution of the outcome.

#### Intersectionality

Single-variable subgroup analysis can miss important effects.

For example, an intervention may work well for high-income users and poorly for low-income users with disabilities. Neither income nor disability analysis alone necessarily reveals that.

However, indiscriminate slicing creates statistical and privacy problems. Subgroup analysis should therefore be hypothesis-driven and sufficiently powered.

#### Power should be measured separately

A particularly important extension is distribution of decision-making power.

Who gets to:

- deploy AI?
- set its objectives?
- collect the data?
- determine acceptable errors?
- appeal decisions?
- switch the system off?
- capture the financial gains?

An intervention can improve individual outcomes while simultaneously increasing institutional dependence or concentrating power.

That may be a socially important effect even when difficult to express numerically.

#### Severe harms need non-compensatory treatment

I would reject a framework in which any harm can simply be offset by enough benefits elsewhere.

Some outcomes may have thresholds or rights-like constraints.

For example, systematic denial of basic procedural rights should not necessarily become acceptable because an AI system generates sufficient aggregate economic value.

This means the eventual architecture may need both:

- compensatory measures, where gains and losses can reasonably be compared; and
- constraints/floors, below which certain harms trigger a negative determination regardless of aggregate gains.

### 6. Time

AI effects unfold on very different timescales.

I would explicitly distinguish:

Immediate: hours to months;  
Transitional: months to several years;  
Long-term: years to decades;  
Intergenerational: effects on people not yet participating in the present decision.

These categories should not be treated as arbitrary universal intervals. The relevant horizon depends on the intervention.

#### Temporal profiles

Instead of one outcome value, an assessment could contain a trajectory:

initial cost → transition → short-term benefit → uncertain long-term effect

This is much more informative than a single present-value estimate.

For example, an AI system might initially displace workers but eventually create new employment, or it might initially improve efficiency while gradually degrading institutional expertise.

#### Option value and irreversibility

Some AI effects deserve special treatment because they are difficult to reverse.

Examples include:

- institutional dependence;
- erosion of human expertise;
- loss of privacy;
- irreversible environmental damage;
- changes to critical infrastructure;
- persistent labor-market restructuring.

The framework should therefore track reversibility alongside magnitude.

A modest but irreversible harm may warrant more attention than a larger reversible inconvenience.

#### Discounting

Welfare economics offers established methods for discounting future effects, but discount rates contain normative assumptions.

I would not bury these assumptions in a technical parameter.

If future generations matter substantially, different discounting choices can materially alter conclusions. Therefore the framework should show results under multiple defensible assumptions.

#### Fragility

One underdeveloped category deserves emphasis: resilience.

An AI deployment can produce impressive efficiency while making a system more fragile.

Examples might include dependence on:

- a single provider;
- a particular model;
- centralized infrastructure;
- proprietary data;
- a narrow pool of human expertise.

Thus an AI intervention's impact profile should include not merely “does it work?” but:

What happens if it fails, disappears, becomes unaffordable, or behaves differently than expected?

### 7. Aggregation and presentation

My preferred initial form is a multidimensional impact profile with an evidence layer, not a single score.

A useful profile might contain:

| Layer | Question |
|---|---|
| Exposure | Where and how is AI being used? |
| Outcomes | What changed for people? |
| Attribution | How much can reasonably be attributed to AI? |
| Distribution | Who gained and who lost? |
| Time | When do effects occur? |
| Harm/risk | What adverse outcomes occurred or could occur? |
| Evidence | How strong is the evidence? |
| Uncertainty | What remains unknown? |
| Power | Who controls the intervention and its consequences? |

The table is conceptual rather than a proposed final reporting format.

#### Why not one number?

A composite score necessarily chooses:

- which outcomes count;
- how much they count;
- how harms trade off against benefits;
- how inequality is treated;
- how future effects are discounted;
- how uncertainty is handled.

Those are partly ethical and political choices.

A number can conceal those choices.

#### Could a composite still be useful?

Yes, but only under constrained conditions.

For example, a government might need a budgetary or policy comparison across interventions. A composite could be generated from a published weighting model.

But I would require:

- the underlying multidimensional data remain visible;
- weights are publicly specified;
- alternative weighting schemes are shown;
- distributional constraints are not erased;
- uncertainty propagates into the composite;
- users cannot interpret the number as an objective measurement of “goodness.”

The score should be an output of an explicit value model, not a discovered natural constant.

#### A potentially better name

“AI Human Benefit Index” may prematurely commit the project to both “benefit” and “index.”

I would initially prefer something like:

AI Human Impact Observatory (AI-HIO)

or

AI Human Outcomes Framework (AI-HOF).

“Impact” is broader and allows negative effects. “Outcomes” emphasizes what happens to people rather than what the technology claims to do.

If the research ultimately establishes that a defensible composite is possible, “AI Human Benefit Index” could become an output rather than the project's foundational premise.

### 8. The role of AI in measurement

AI itself could be extremely useful in constructing this evidence base.

Legitimate applications include:

- extracting outcome information from large bodies of records;
- linking heterogeneous datasets;
- coding qualitative interviews;
- detecting changes in patterns;
- identifying potential confounders;
- generating hypotheses;
- monitoring longitudinal indicators;
- identifying missing evidence;
- assisting systematic reviews;
- translating or harmonizing measurements across languages;
- detecting anomalous reporting;
- helping researchers explore alternative causal models.

But the distinction between AI as measurement instrument and AI as evaluator of AI must remain sharp.

#### Circularity risk

An AI company should not be allowed to demonstrate its own social benefit primarily through an AI system that evaluates its outputs using criteria designed by the same organization.

That is analogous to an organization grading its own exam.

AI can assist analysis, but consequential conclusions should retain:

- independent human oversight;
- reproducible methods;
- inspectable data provenance;
- adversarial review;
- non-AI validation where feasible.

#### Surveillance risk

Measuring human outcomes can become an excuse to collect unprecedented quantities of behavioral data.

The framework should therefore adopt a data minimization principle:

Do not collect intimate information merely because it makes the index more statistically convenient.

Measurement should prefer:

- consent where appropriate;
- aggregation;
- privacy-preserving methods;
- secure data environments;
- purpose limitation;
- retention limits.

#### Automation bias

Researchers may over-trust AI-generated interpretations, especially when they are fluent and quantitative.

AI-generated evidence should therefore carry provenance and confidence information, and important findings should be independently reproducible.

#### Manipulation

If organizations know exactly what the evaluator rewards, they will eventually optimize for the evaluator.

That is not hypothetical in principle; it is a basic problem of measurement systems.

AI should therefore be used more for evidence discovery and anomaly detection than as an unquestioned scoring authority.

### 9. Governance and resistance to gaming

The greatest threat may not be methodological error but institutional capture.

An AI-impact framework could easily become:

- corporate ESG-style marketing;
- a national competitiveness ranking;
- a government legitimacy instrument;
- an investor-facing metric;
- an advocacy tool;
- an optimization target for AI companies.

The governance structure should anticipate this from the beginning.

#### Who should govern it?

I would favor a multi-stakeholder independent institution, with separation among:

- methodological researchers;
- affected communities;
- statisticians and measurement experts;
- economists;
- social scientists;
- domain specialists;
- civil-society organizations;
- disability and accessibility experts;
- labor representatives;
- technology experts;
- policymakers;
- independent auditors.

No single stakeholder group should control both the definition of benefit and the evaluation of benefit.

#### Auditing

There should be at least three distinct audits:

Methodological audit: Are the measurements and causal methods defensible?

Data audit: Are the underlying data complete, representative, and accurately processed?

Distributional/impact audit: Are important affected groups or harms systematically omitted?

A fourth category could be a gaming audit: Can an organization improve its measured score without improving the underlying human outcome?

#### Contestability

People and institutions affected by a measurement should have mechanisms to contest:

- data;
- methodology;
- subgroup characterization;
- causal interpretation;
- weighting;
- omissions.

The framework should have a documented revision process rather than presenting its categories as permanent scientific facts.

#### Resist leaderboard logic

I would strongly discourage country, company, or model leaderboards during the research phase.

A leaderboard invites organizations to optimize for rank rather than reality.

Instead, the framework should produce case-specific impact profiles and comparative studies where comparison is substantively justified.

#### Goodhart's law

Once a measure becomes a target, it tends to cease functioning as a good measure.

The practical response should not be to abandon measurement. It should be to design for measurement pluralism and adversarial evaluation.

Use multiple outcome measures, periodically revise them, retain qualitative evidence, and deliberately investigate whether improvements in the metric correspond to improvements in lived experience.

Most importantly:

The framework should measure whether its own measurement process predicts or corresponds to actual human outcomes.

The evaluator itself needs evaluation.

### 10. Research path and criticism

I would make the first phase much smaller than the eventual ambition suggests.

#### A credible minimum viable research program

##### Phase 1: Conceptual specification

Select perhaps 5–10 concrete AI interventions, deliberately heterogeneous.

For example:

- an AI-supported clinical application;
- an educational application;
- a workplace productivity system;
- a public-sector decision-support system;
- an accessibility application;
- a scientific or research application;
- a consumer-facing generative AI use case.

Do not attempt to measure “AI's impact on society” initially.

For each case, construct a theory of change:

AI capability → changed activity/decision → intermediate outcome → human outcome → distributional/system effects.

Then identify which links can actually be observed.

##### Phase 2: Outcome inventory

For each case, distinguish:

Inputs: investment, compute, staffing.

Capabilities: benchmark performance, reliability.

Exposure: users, transactions, deployment.

Activities: tasks performed, decisions changed.

Intermediate outcomes: speed, error rates, productivity.

Human outcomes: health, income, learning, well-being, autonomy, safety, etc.

System effects: labor markets, institutions, inequality, resilience, environmental effects.

This separation alone would correct much of the current tendency to equate deployment with benefit.

##### Phase 3: Counterfactual studies

For a subset of cases, conduct genuinely causal evaluations.

Where randomization is ethical and practical, use it.

Where it is not, use strong quasi-experimental designs.

Where neither is possible, use contribution analysis and explicitly downgrade causal confidence.

##### Phase 4: Distributional analysis

For every case, ask whether aggregate outcomes conceal divergent effects.

This should be mandatory rather than optional.

##### Phase 5: Longitudinal follow-up

At least some cases should be followed long enough to detect:

- adaptation;
- rebound effects;
- deskilling;
- substitution;
- institutional dependence;
- delayed benefits;
- delayed harms.

##### Phase 6: Compare measurement methods

The research should itself be an experiment in measurement.

Have different research teams independently assess the same cases.

Then ask:

How much do conclusions change because of definitions, datasets, causal models, and value assumptions?

That would expose methodological instability before the project turns it into an official index.

#### Existing intellectual traditions to borrow from

The project should not be built as though AI measurement were an entirely new scientific discipline.

Useful foundations include:

- causal inference and program evaluation;
- randomized controlled trials;
- quasi-experimental methods;
- health technology assessment;
- health outcomes and quality-of-life measurement;
- welfare economics;
- the capability approach;
- human-development measurement;
- social accounting and national statistics;
- labor economics;
- environmental impact assessment;
- technology assessment;
- science and technology studies;
- risk analysis;
- distributional impact assessment;
- systematic reviews and evidence grading;
- human-rights and rights-impact assessment;
- responsible innovation;
- participatory research.

The challenge is integration, not invention of every component from scratch.

#### Data and expertise required

A credible program would require substantially more than AI researchers.

It would need:

- statisticians;
- causal-inference researchers;
- economists;
- epidemiologists where relevant;
- psychologists and behavioral scientists;
- sociologists;
- labor researchers;
- disability and accessibility researchers;

#### Strongest objections

##### Objection 1: Human welfare is too plural to measure

This is the strongest philosophical objection.

There may be no non-arbitrary way to compare autonomy, income, health, knowledge, relationships, and environmental quality.

My response: Do not pretend there is one. A multidimensional framework can be useful without resolving all value disagreements.

##### Objection 2: Causal attribution will often be impossible

For large-scale AI effects, clean counterfactuals may not exist.

My response: Correct. The framework should distinguish causal knowledge from contribution evidence and preserve unattributed effects.

##### Objection 3: Measurement will create perverse incentives

Once organizations optimize for the metric, the metric becomes gameable.

My response: This is a reason for plural, auditable, revisable measurement—not for a single permanent score.

##### Objection 4: The framework will smuggle in political values

Deciding whose welfare matters, how inequality should be treated, and how future generations count are normative decisions.

My response: Those decisions should be made explicit and contestable rather than hidden inside technical weighting formulas.

##### Objection 5: The measurement infrastructure could become surveillance infrastructure

A serious human-impact measurement system could require sensitive information about people's lives.

My response: This is a fundamental constraint. Privacy should be treated as part of the framework's legitimacy, not merely as an implementation issue.

##### Objection 6: AI effects are endogenous to society

AI does not act on a static population. People adapt, firms react, institutions change, and other technologies evolve.

My response: Exactly. This suggests longitudinal and system-level analysis rather than simple before/after measurement.

##### Objection 7: “Benefit” privileges humans and present values

AI may affect animals, ecosystems, institutions, and future generations.

My response: A human-benefit project can deliberately define its scope as human welfare, but it should explicitly document what that scope excludes. Environmental and intergenerational effects should at least be captured where they affect human welfare, with room for a broader successor framework.

#### A more fundamental alternative

I would consider whether the actual research question should be:

“How can societies develop credible evidence about the human consequences of AI interventions?”

That is more modest than “measure AI's human benefit,” but potentially more scientifically defensible.

It permits findings such as:

- substantial benefit with strong evidence;
- benefit with significant distributional concerns;
- mixed effects;
- no detectable effect;
- plausible harm;
- insufficient evidence.

That vocabulary may be more valuable than forcing every case onto a positive-to-negative numerical scale.

The project could still use “AI Human Benefit Index” as a deliberately provisional working title, but I would not make the index its ontological commitment.

This response is based on existing knowledge rather than a fresh literature review, as requested. Consequently, I have deliberately avoided claims about the current state of particular empirical datasets, recent AI-impact studies, or the newest institutional initiatives. The methodological traditions named above are established bodies of work, but I have not attempted here to establish which specific frameworks, standards, or organizations currently provide the best implementation for each component. ### Provisional recommendation

The most credible form of this inquiry, in my view, is not initially an AI Human Benefit Index.

It should begin as an AI Human Outcomes and Impact Observatory: a multidisciplinary, case-based, longitudinal research program that measures actual human outcomes, attempts causal or contribution-based attribution, reports distributions and harms, preserves uncertainty, and makes normative choices explicit.

Its basic unit should usually be the AI-enabled intervention in a defined context and population, not “the AI model” or “AI” in the abstract.

Its principal output should be a multidimensional impact profile with evidence grades and uncertainty, supplemented by rigorous case studies. A composite index should be treated as an experimental downstream representation, not the foundation.

The most important methodological innovation would therefore not be a clever formula. It would be the insistence on keeping separate:

capability → exposure → activity → intermediate outcome → human outcome → attribution → distribution → time → uncertainty.

That separation would make it considerably harder to mistake technological activity for social benefit.

### Three design principles that should not be compromised

1. **Measure lived outcomes, not technological activity.**  
   Adoption, investment, benchmark performance, usage, and productivity are evidence about mechanisms or exposure. They are not substitutes for evidence about what happened to people.

2. **Never hide uncertainty, distribution, or normative judgment behind a single number.**  
   If causal attribution is weak, say so. If effects differ radically across groups, show it. If a weighting system is normative, expose the weights.

3. **Design the measurement system to resist its own success.**  
   Assume that organizations will eventually optimize against whatever is measured. Independent governance, adversarial auditing, methodological plurality, contestability, periodic revision, and qualitative evidence should therefore be architectural features rather than safeguards added later.

### Three questions that remain genuinely unresolved

1. **Is there a defensible way to compare fundamentally different human outcomes without imposing a controversial theory of what a good human life is?**  
   Multidimensional reporting avoids some of the problem, but it does not eliminate it whenever decisions require tradeoffs.

2. **How far can meaningful causal attribution extend once AI becomes a general-purpose infrastructure embedded throughout an economy or society?**  
   At some scale, the relevant counterfactual may cease to be “the same world without AI” in any empirically tractable sense.

3. **Can a human-impact measurement system remain useful after it becomes powerful enough to influence behavior?**  
   This is ultimately a reflexivity problem: the more authoritative the measurement becomes, the more incentives it creates to manipulate both the underlying reality and the measurement process itself.

The most important conclusion from this first conceptual pass is therefore somewhat counterintuitive: the success condition may not be producing a number called the AI Human Benefit Index. It may be producing a measurement institution capable of saying, with disciplined evidence, when we know that AI helped people, when it harmed them, when its effects were distributed unequally, and when we simply do not know.
