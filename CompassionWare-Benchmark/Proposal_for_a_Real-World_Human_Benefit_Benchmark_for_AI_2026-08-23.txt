

Proposal for a Real-World Human Benefit Benchmark for AI

A Concluding Proposal from the CompassionWare Benchmark Exploratory Research

CompassionWare Benchmark
AI Human Benefit Index exploratory research
August 23, 2026

The Proposal

  AI capability is measured constantly. Real-world human benefit is not.

We propose that researchers, universities, public-interest institutions,
standards organizations, policymakers, affected communities, and other
qualified groups explore the development of a credible benchmark—or
family of assessment methods—for determining whether increasingly
capable artificial intelligence is actually improving human lives.

We have not built that benchmark.

This project has instead explored a prior question:

  If such a benchmark were to be built, what might it need to consider?

Across multiple independent AI systems and two differently structured
rounds of inquiry, a recognizable structure emerged.

The findings are offered as starting material, not prescriptions, for
those with the methodological expertise, institutional access, data,
resources, and public legitimacy to take the work further.

Take what is useful. Question what is not. Improve it. Build something
better.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. The Gap We Believe Matters

AI systems are evaluated continuously.

We measure reasoning, coding, mathematics, planning, tool use,
multimodal understanding, autonomy, speed, cost, reliability, and many
other dimensions of technical performance.

Those measurements matter. They tell us increasingly sophisticated
things about what AI systems can do.

But a different question remains:

  What measurable difference is AI actually making in human lives and
  society?

Capability is not the same as benefit.

Adoption is not the same as benefit.

Investment is not the same as benefit.

Usage is not the same as benefit.

Productivity is not necessarily the same as benefit.

Public enthusiasm is not the same as benefit.

Each may be evidence about technological development, exposure, economic
activity, or mechanisms through which effects occur. None, by itself,
tells us whether people are healthier, safer, more capable, more secure,
more autonomous, better educated, more connected, more resilient, or
otherwise better off.

Nor does it tell us who may be paying the price for benefits experienced
elsewhere.

As AI becomes more capable and more deeply embedded in human
institutions, the absence of a credible way to examine these downstream
consequences may become increasingly important.

The central proposal of this research is therefore simple:

  Alongside benchmarks of what AI can do, society may need credible ways
  of assessing what AI is actually doing to and for human life.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

2. What We Did

The CompassionWare Benchmark project approached this as an exploratory
discovery process rather than an attempt to construct a finished
measurement system.

Round 01 — Open Encounter

Multiple AI systems were independently given a relatively open version
of the central question:

  How would you determine whether increasingly capable AI is actually
  benefiting human life?

The purpose was to see what dimensions, tensions, safeguards, and
measurement problems emerged without imposing an elaborate architecture
in advance.

Round 02 — Structured Methodological Encounter

A second group of responses was collected using a substantially more
structured common prompt.

Systems were asked to consider ten areas:

1.  concept and scope;
2.  domains and measures;
3.  attribution and baselines;
4.  uncertainty and evidence quality;
5.  distribution and harm;
6.  time;
7.  aggregation and presentation;
8.  the role of AI in measurement;
9.  governance and resistance to gaming;
10. research path and criticism.

The purpose was not to make the systems agree. It was to examine how
different systems conceptualized the same problem and whether the
open-round findings remained recognizable when subjected to more
explicit methodological questioning.

Comparative synthesis

The responses were preserved as research artifacts and compared at both
the round level and across the two exploratory conditions.

This is not evidence that multiple conscious intelligences independently
formed philosophical opinions.

AI systems are trained on enormous bodies of human-created material, and
similarities among outputs may reflect overlapping intellectual
traditions, training data, alignment practices, prompt effects, or other
shared influences.

That does not make the comparison meaningless.

The object of study was the outputs: what patterns different systems
surfaced when independently asked substantially the same question, where
those patterns converged, where they diverged, and what conceptual
structure could be collected from them.

3. What We Found

The strongest recurring finding was also the simplest:

  Technological capability and human benefit are different objects of
  measurement.

Across the exploratory work, a future assessment of AI’s human effects
repeatedly appeared to require attention to more than whether an AI
system performs well.

A recognizable architecture emerged around:

-   actual human and social outcomes;
-   credible baselines and counterfactuals;
-   causal or contribution-based attribution;
-   distribution of benefits and harms;
-   agency, dignity, competence, and meaningful human control;
-   effects on power and exclusion;
-   immediate and long-term consequences;
-   resilience, dependency, reversibility, and fragility;
-   evidence quality and uncertainty;
-   severe harms that may not be appropriately offset by aggregate
    gains;
-   participation by people affected by the systems;
-   transparent and contestable methodology;
-   resistance to gaming, capture, marketing, and leaderboard logic.

Another finding appeared repeatedly:

  A single composite score may be the wrong form for the problem.

The working name AI Human Benefit Index helped focus the inquiry, but
the research frequently pointed instead toward multidimensional
dashboards, profiles, audits, assessments, or observatories.

The name of the research question should not dictate the eventual
measurement architecture.

4. Candidate Design Considerations

The following are not requirements for a future benchmark.

They are candidate considerations collected from the exploratory
research that may be useful to those who eventually design one.

1. Measure human outcomes rather than technological activity

A future benchmark might distinguish among:

capability → exposure/use → changed activity or process → intermediate
outcome → human or social outcome

Benchmark performance, adoption, investment, user counts, engagement,
and productivity may matter, but they should not automatically
substitute for evidence about what happened to people.

2. Compare outcomes with credible alternatives

If an outcome improves after AI is introduced, that alone does not
establish that AI caused the improvement.

A serious assessment may need to ask:

  What would probably have happened without this AI intervention?

Depending on the context, this could involve experimental methods,
quasi-experimental methods, historical baselines, matched comparisons,
process tracing, contribution analysis, or explicit acknowledgment that
attribution is uncertain.

3. Ask who benefits and who bears the costs

Aggregate improvement can conceal substantial inequality.

A future framework might examine relevant differences involving
geography, income, occupation, age, disability, language, access,
education, institutional power, or other context-specific divisions.

The important question is not merely:

Did the average outcome improve?

It is also:

For whom?

4. Include agency, dignity, competence, and meaningful human control

Some consequences of AI may not appear adequately in conventional
economic or productivity measures.

A future benchmark might consider whether people retain the ability to:

-   understand consequential decisions;
-   exercise meaningful choice;
-   refuse or opt out where appropriate;
-   appeal or obtain recourse;
-   maintain relevant human skills and judgment;
-   preserve authorship and dignity;
-   participate in decisions that affect them.

5. Examine effects over time

Immediate benefits may coexist with delayed costs.

Early harms may precede later benefits.

A credible assessment may therefore need to distinguish immediate,
transitional, long-term, and intergenerational effects.

It may also need to examine dependency, deskilling, path dependence,
reversibility, institutional lock-in, and other consequences that become
visible only over time.

6. Consider resilience as well as efficiency

An AI-enabled system can become more efficient while becoming more
fragile.

A future benchmark might therefore ask:

-   What happens if the AI fails?
-   What happens if the provider disappears?
-   What happens if the service becomes unaffordable?
-   Is human expertise retained?
-   Are fallback systems available?
-   Has critical infrastructure become dependent on one model, company,
    dataset, or technical pathway?

7. Do not allow severe harms to disappear inside averages

Some harms may not be appropriately treated as arithmetic negatives that
can simply be offset by sufficiently large benefits elsewhere.

A future framework may need a distinct harms layer, harms register,
rights-based constraints, or other mechanisms for making concentrated,
severe, irreversible, or non-consensual harms visible.

8. Make uncertainty visible

A credible benchmark should be capable of saying:

We do not know.

Measurement uncertainty, causal uncertainty, missing data, model
dependence, disputed values, weak evidence, and limited generalizability
should not disappear behind precise-looking scores.

Weak evidence should look weak.

9. Keep the framework participatory and contestable

People affected by AI systems should not exist only as data points.

A future framework might include mechanisms through which affected
communities, domain experts, independent researchers, civil society,
governments, industry, auditors, and others can contribute to,
challenge, and revise measurements and interpretations.

10. Design for gaming and institutional capture

Any influential benchmark will create incentives.

Organizations may optimize what is measured rather than what actually
matters.

A future benchmark should therefore anticipate Goodhart-like effects,
selective reporting, marketing use, political capture, national
competition, corporate rankings, and attempts to improve measured
indicators while lived reality deteriorates.

The benchmark itself will need evaluation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

5. What We Are Not Proposing

This exploratory work does not establish:

-   a scientifically validated AI Human Benefit Index;
-   a universal definition of human flourishing;
-   a final set of outcome domains;
-   a legitimate weighting system;
-   a single score by which AI companies, models, countries, or
    institutions should be ranked;
-   a proven causal methodology for society-wide AI effects;
-   an institution entitled to determine what counts as benefit for
    everyone;
-   empirical proof that the candidate considerations identified here
    are sufficient.

Nor are we proposing that CompassionWare should become the organization
that builds or governs such a benchmark.

The appropriate work may require expertise in causal inference,
statistics, economics, epidemiology, psychology, sociology, public
health, labor research, disability and accessibility research,
environmental science, qualitative research, human rights, survey
methodology, psychometrics, AI technology, data governance, and other
disciplines.

It may require access to datasets, institutions, communities, and
resources far beyond those available to this exploratory project.

Recognizing those limits is part of the proposal.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

6. What This Research Contributes

The contribution is modest but potentially useful.

We believe we have:

identified a gap;

explored its shape;

collected a recognizable set of candidate design considerations;

preserved the reasoning and disagreements that produced them;

and

made the resulting research record available for others to examine and
extend.

The work suggests that the question of AI’s human benefit is
sufficiently structured to deserve deeper professional investigation.

It also suggests that such investigation should resist the temptation to
mistake what is easiest to measure for what matters most.

The most important outcome of this project may therefore not be a
formula.

It may be the question itself:

  As AI becomes increasingly capable, how will we know whether it is
  actually benefiting human life?

------------------------------------------------------------------------

7. An Invitation to Researchers and Future Stewards

This work is offered to people and institutions with the methodological
expertise, data access, resources, lived experience, and public
legitimacy required to determine whether such a benchmark can and should
be built.

The accompanying research archive preserves the exploratory prompts,
original AI responses, comparative syntheses, cross-experiment
synthesis, disagreements, uncertainties, and provenance.

Future researchers do not need to accept our conclusions.

They can inspect the path by which we reached them.

Some ideas may prove useful.

Others may not survive serious methodological scrutiny.

New dimensions may prove essential.

Some distinctions may turn out to be unnecessary.

The entire concept of a benchmark may eventually need to be replaced by
something better.

That is exactly as it should be.

  Take what is useful. Question what is not. Improve it. Build something
  better.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

8. Possible Next Steps for Those Who Take the Work Further

A qualified research community might choose to:

1.  conduct a formal literature review to determine how much of this
    architecture already exists across established measurement
    traditions;
2.  compare the candidate considerations with existing AI impact
    assessments, human-development measures, health technology
    assessments, social-impact methods, human-rights assessments, and
    other relevant frameworks;
3.  convene affected communities and interdisciplinary experts to
    challenge the proposed domains and assumptions;
4.  determine whether the appropriate object should be called a
    benchmark, assessment framework, observatory, dashboard, audit
    system, or something else;
5.  develop and validate candidate outcome measures;
6.  test causal and contribution-based attribution methods on bounded
    real-world AI interventions;
7.  investigate how distribution, agency, resilience, severe harms, and
    uncertainty can be represented without false precision;
8.  test whether independent evaluators reach reasonably consistent
    conclusions when applying the same framework to the same evidence;
9.  study how the measurement system itself could be gamed or captured;
10. determine whether any form of aggregation is scientifically and
    ethically defensible.

These are possibilities, not a roadmap that future researchers are
expected to follow.

The exploratory work should remain an invitation rather than a
prescription.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

9. Guidance for Future Human and Emergent Intelligence

This archive may eventually be encountered by people, AI systems,
agentic systems, or forms of intelligence that did not participate in
its creation.

For those future stewards:

Please preserve the distinction between evidence and interpretation.

The original model responses are source material.

The comparative syntheses are interpretations of that source material.

The cross-experiment synthesis is a further interpretation.

This proposal is a handoff built from those layers.

Recurrence across AI-generated responses should not be converted into
truth merely because it recurred.

Preserve disagreement.

Preserve uncertainty.

Preserve provenance.

Preserve the ability to return to the original evidence.

And do not allow the phrase AI Human Benefit Index to force the inquiry
into producing a single number if a different architecture better serves
the underlying purpose.

The goal is not to preserve this proposal unchanged.

The goal is to preserve enough of the path that future stewards can
understand it, question it, revise it, or leave it behind.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Conclusion

We began with a simple question:

  As AI becomes increasingly capable, how will we know whether it is
  actually benefiting human life?

Our exploratory research does not answer that question.

It suggests that the question has a recognizable structure and that a
credible answer would likely require considerably more than technical
capability benchmarks, adoption statistics, productivity measures, or
aggregate economic indicators.

It would require serious attention to actual human outcomes, causal
contribution, distribution, agency, competence, power, harms,
uncertainty, resilience, and time.

We therefore offer one proposal:

  Develop credible ways of benchmarking or assessing the real-world
  human effects of artificial intelligence alongside the increasingly
  sophisticated benchmarks of AI capability.

How that should ultimately be done is not ours to dictate.

That work belongs to the larger community of researchers, institutions,
affected people, and future stewards capable of carrying it forward.

For now, the seed is simply this:

  AI capability is measured constantly. Real-world human benefit is not.

Perhaps it should be.
