# Common Exploratory Prompt

We are beginning an exploratory research experiment about how the real-world human effects of artificial intelligence might be measured.

AI systems are commonly evaluated for capabilities such as reasoning, coding, planning, tool use, and autonomy. Separate behavioral evaluations may ask how a system handles judgment, uncertainty, power, restraint, or competing values. This inquiry concerns a third and distinct question:

What measurable difference is AI actually making in human lives and society?

The provisional working name is the AI Human Benefit Index (AI-HBI). Neither the name, the idea of an “index,” nor the assumption that a single composite measure is desirable should be treated as settled.

Please approach this as an independent research collaborator. We are giving substantially the same prompt to several AI systems because we want to compare how each one conceptualizes the problem—not make them agree. Do not try to infer or reproduce a preferred CompassionWare answer. Challenge the framing wherever warranted, and preserve important ambiguity rather than manufacturing false completeness.

Develop your own rigorous approach to the following questions:

1. Concept and scope: How should “real-world human benefit from AI” be defined? What should the unit or units of analysis be—for example, a system, application, intervention, organization, sector, national ecosystem, or society-wide pattern? Is an index the right form at all?
2. Domains and measures: What dimensions of human life should be examined, and why? For each major dimension you propose, identify plausible outcome measures and distinguish actual human outcomes from activity, adoption, capability, publicity, or investment proxies.
3. Attribution and baselines: How could the framework estimate AI’s contribution when outcomes have many causes? Discuss appropriate baselines, counterfactuals, causal or contribution-based methods, confounding factors, and what should remain explicitly unattributed.
4. Uncertainty and evidence quality: How should uncertainty, missing data, disputed values, measurement error, model dependence, and varying evidence quality be represented without creating an appearance of precision that the evidence cannot support?
5. Distribution and harm: How should the framework show who benefits, who bears costs, who is excluded, and whether aggregate gains conceal concentrated or severe harms? Include relevant geographic, economic, demographic, disability, and power-related differences without assuming that these are the only important divisions.
6. Time: How should immediate, transitional, long-term, and intergenerational effects be distinguished? How should the framework handle benefits that arrive later but impose costs now, or early gains that create longer-term fragility or harm?
7. Aggregation and presentation: Should findings be combined into a single score, presented as a multidimensional dashboard or profile, or expressed in another form? Explain what would be lost or distorted by your preferred approach and how value judgments or weighting decisions should be made visible.
8. The role of AI in measurement: What legitimate roles could AI itself play in gathering, linking, interpreting, or monitoring the evidence? What safeguards would be needed to address bias, opacity, circular self-evaluation, surveillance, manipulation, data quality, and conflicts of interest?
9. Governance and resistance to gaming: Who should define, maintain, audit, contest, and revise the framework? How could it resist becoming a marketing device, company or national leaderboard, political instrument, or optimization target that improves the measured indicators while worsening lived reality?
10. Research path and criticism: What would a credible minimum viable research program look like? Identify useful existing measurement traditions or disciplines, the data and expertise required, the strongest objections to the project, and any alternative framing or name you believe would be better.

Please organize your response under these ten headings so the responses can be compared, but use whatever conceptual structure you think is strongest within each heading. Clearly distinguish established methods or frameworks from your own proposals. Do not invent evidence, statistics, studies, or citations. End with:

* your provisional recommendation for the most credible form this inquiry should take;
* three design principles that should not be compromised; and
* three questions that remain genuinely unresolved.

For this first conceptual round, do not browse the web or use external tools. Base the response on your existing knowledge and state any knowledge limitations that materially affect your answer.

# Mistral Response

This is a rich and vital inquiry. The ambition to measure AI’s real-world human benefit—rather than just its capabilities or behaviors—is both necessary and fraught with conceptual and practical challenges. Below, I offer a rigorous, independent approach to each of your ten questions, organized as requested. My aim is to surface tensions, propose actionable frameworks, and preserve ambiguity where it is most productive.

---

## 1. Concept and scope

**Definition:**
“Real-world human benefit from AI” should be defined as **the net improvement in human well-being, agency, and dignity that can be plausibly attributed to the deployment of AI systems, as experienced by affected individuals and communities**. This definition centers lived experience, avoids reducing benefit to economic or technical metrics, and explicitly includes both positive and negative effects.

**Unit of Analysis:**
The unit should be **the AI-mediated intervention**—a specific deployment of AI in a context (e.g., a diagnostic tool in a hospital, a chatbot for mental health support, an algorithmic hiring system). This allows for granularity and comparability across use cases. However, the framework must also aggregate up to **sectors, populations, and ecosystems** to capture systemic effects (e.g., labor market shifts, environmental impacts).

**Is an index the right form?**
An index is not inherently the right form. A single composite score risks obscuring trade-offs, masking distributional effects, and implying a false precision. A **multidimensional dashboard** or **narrative profile** may better capture the complexity, but even these can be misused. The form should follow the function: if the goal is to inform policy, a dashboard with clear, disaggregated metrics may be most useful; if the goal is public accountability, a narrative report with highlighted trade-offs may be more appropriate.

---

## 2. Domains and measures

**Core Dimensions of Human Life:**
- **Health and well-being** (physical, mental, social)
- **Economic security and opportunity** (income, employment quality, access to resources)
- **Autonomy and agency** (control over one’s life, freedom from coercion or manipulation)
- **Dignity and respect** (recognition, fairness, absence of stigma or discrimination)
- **Social cohesion and trust** (community bonds, institutional trust, reduced polarization)
- **Environmental sustainability** (impact on ecosystems, resource use, climate)
- **Cultural and creative expression** (access to and participation in cultural life)

**Plausible Outcome Measures:**
For each dimension, measures should be **direct outcomes** (e.g., changes in life expectancy, self-reported well-being, income stability, access to healthcare) rather than proxies (e.g., number of AI systems deployed, investment in AI, user engagement). For example:
- **Health:** Reduction in preventable deaths, improvement in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), patient-reported outcomes.
- **Economic security:** Changes in wage levels, job stability, access to credit or education.
- **Autonomy:** User control over data, ability to contest AI decisions, freedom from algorithmic manipulation.
- **Dignity:** Reduction in discriminatory outcomes, increased representation, user satisfaction with respectful treatment.

**Distinguishing Outcomes from Proxies:**
Proxies (e.g., “number of AI startups,” “adoption rates,” “model accuracy”) are only useful if they can be empirically linked to outcomes. The framework should explicitly flag and avoid relying on proxies unless a strong causal or correlational case can be made.

---

## 3. Attribution and baselines

**Estimating AI’s Contribution:**
- **Counterfactual analysis:** Compare outcomes in the presence of AI to what would have happened in its absence. This can be done via randomized controlled trials (RCTs), difference-in-differences, synthetic controls, or other quasi-experimental methods.
- **Contribution analysis:** Use statistical methods (e.g., mediation analysis, structural causal models) to estimate the proportion of observed change attributable to AI, controlling for other factors.
- **Qualitative attribution:** For complex, long-term effects, use case studies, process tracing, and expert judgment to assess AI’s role.

**Baselines:**
- **Static baselines:** Pre-AI deployment conditions.
- **Dynamic baselines:** What would have happened without AI, accounting for trends (e.g., technological progress, policy changes).
- **Comparative baselines:** Outcomes in similar contexts where AI was not deployed.

**Confounding Factors:**
- **Selection bias:** AI is often deployed in contexts where change is already expected (e.g., high-growth sectors).
- **Simultaneity:** AI may be both a cause and a consequence of other changes (e.g., digital infrastructure, policy shifts).
- **Interaction effects:** AI’s impact may depend on complementary factors (e.g., user training, institutional capacity).

**Unattributed Effects:**
Some outcomes should remain explicitly unattributed if the evidence is weak or the causal chain is too complex. The framework should be transparent about what is known, unknown, and unknowable.

---

## 4. Uncertainty and evidence quality

**Representing Uncertainty:**
- **Confidence intervals:** For quantitative estimates, provide ranges and probabilities.
- **Evidence grades:** Use a system (e.g., GRADE) to rate the quality of evidence (high, moderate, low, very low) based on study design, consistency, directness, and precision.
- **Narrative uncertainty:** For qualitative findings, explicitly state the strength of the evidence and the degree of consensus among experts.

**Measurement Error:**
- Acknowledge and, where possible, quantify measurement error (e.g., survey bias, data gaps).
- Use sensitivity analyses to show how results change under different assumptions.

**Model Dependence:**
- If AI models are used to generate or analyze data, disclose their role and limitations.
- Avoid circularity (e.g., using AI to measure its own benefits).

**Varying Evidence Quality:**
- The framework should be **tiered**, with the strongest evidence given the most weight and the weakest evidence clearly labeled as provisional or speculative.

---
## 5. Distribution and harm

**Who Benefits, Who Bears Costs:**
- **Disaggregation:** Break down outcomes by relevant dimensions (e.g., geography, income, gender, race, disability, age, digital literacy).
- **Intersectionality:** Recognize that individuals may belong to multiple marginalized groups, and their experiences may not be captured by single-axis analysis.
- **Power dynamics:** Explicitly analyze who has the power to deploy, shape, or resist AI, and how this affects outcomes.

**Concentrated Harms:**
- **Severity:** Distinguish between minor inconveniences and severe, irreversible harms (e.g., loss of livelihood, wrongful imprisonment, psychological trauma).
- **Visibility:** Some harms (e.g., algorithmic bias, surveillance) may be invisible to those not directly affected. The framework must include mechanisms for surfacing these (e.g., community consultations, whistleblower protections).

**Exclusion:**
- Identify populations excluded from AI’s benefits (e.g., those without internet access, those in countries with limited AI infrastructure) and those disproportionately harmed (e.g., gig workers, marginalized communities).

---
## 6. Time

**Temporal Dimensions:**
- **Immediate effects:** Short-term changes (e.g., job displacement, initial health improvements).
- **Transitional effects:** Adjustment periods (e.g., reskilling, adaptation to new tools).
- **Long-term effects:** Sustainable changes (e.g., new industries, cultural shifts).
- **Intergenerational effects:** Impacts on future generations (e.g., environmental degradation, educational access).

**Handling Time Trade-offs:**
- **Discounting:** If future benefits are weighted less than present ones, this should be explicit and justified.
- **Path dependency:** Early gains may lock in long-term harms (e.g., surveillance infrastructure, dependency on proprietary systems). The framework should assess the risk of such path dependencies.
- **Reversibility:** Some harms (e.g., loss of trust, environmental damage) may be irreversible. The framework should flag these as especially concerning.

---
## 7. Aggregation and presentation

**Forms of Presentation:**
- **Dashboard:** A set of disaggregated metrics, with the ability to drill down into specific dimensions, populations, or time periods.
- **Narrative profile:** A qualitative report that tells the story of AI’s impact in a given context, highlighting trade-offs, uncertainties, and contested values.
- **Hybrid approach:** Combine quantitative metrics with narrative context.

**What is Lost or Distorted:**
- **Aggregation:** Combining metrics can obscure trade-offs and distributional effects. For example, an average improvement in income may hide increasing inequality.
- **Weighting:** Any weighting of dimensions or populations involves value judgments. These should be made explicit and contestable.

**Visibility of Value Judgments:**
- The framework should include a **methodology transparency section** that explains how and why certain metrics, weights, or interpretations were chosen, and by whom.

---
## 8. The role of AI in measurement

**Legitimate Roles for AI:**
- **Data collection:** AI can help gather and process large, complex datasets (e.g., satellite imagery, social media, sensor data).
- **Pattern recognition:** AI can identify correlations or anomalies in data that might be missed by human analysts.
- **Simulation:** AI can model counterfactuals or future scenarios to estimate potential impacts.
- **Translation and accessibility:** AI can make data and findings more accessible (e.g., through natural language generation, translation, or visualization).

**Safeguards:**
- **Bias:** AI systems used in measurement may inherit or amplify biases present in their training data or design. Regular audits and diverse development teams are essential.
- **Opacity:** AI-driven insights must be explainable and contestable. “Black box” models should not be used for high-stakes decisions.
- **Circular self-evaluation:** AI should not be used to evaluate its own benefits without independent oversight.
- **Surveillance:** Data collection must respect privacy and consent, with clear limits on how data can be used.
- **Manipulation:** AI should not be used to selectively present or suppress findings to serve particular interests.
- **Data quality:** AI-generated data must be validated against ground truth where possible.
- **Conflicts of interest:** Those deploying AI for measurement should be independent from those with a stake in the outcomes (e.g., AI developers, investors).

---
## 9. Governance and resistance to gaming

**Who Should Define and Maintain the Framework:**
- **Multi-stakeholder governance:** The framework should be developed and overseen by a diverse group, including affected communities, independent researchers, policymakers, and industry representatives.
- **Independence:** The governing body should be structurally and financially independent from AI developers and deployers.
- **Transparency:** The methodology, data, and decision-making processes should be publicly accessible.
- **Contestability:** There should be clear mechanisms for challenging and revising the framework, including appeals processes and regular public consultations.

**Resisting Gaming:**
- **Anti-gaming measures:** Use robust statistical methods (e.g., outlier detection, benchmarking against external data) to detect and discourage manipulation.
- **Qualitative checks:** Supplement quantitative metrics with qualitative assessments (e.g., case studies, community feedback) that are harder to game.
- **Consequence management:** Establish penalties for attempts to game the system (e.g., exclusion from rankings, public naming and shaming).
- **Avoiding league tables:** Refuse to produce or endorse simplified rankings or scores that could be used for marketing or political purposes.

---
## 10. Research path and criticism

**Minimum Viable Research Program:**
- **Phase 1: Scoping and literature review** (6–12 months)
  - Map existing measurement traditions (e.g., development economics, public health, human rights, environmental impact assessment).
  - Identify gaps and overlaps in current AI impact assessments.
- **Phase 2: Pilot studies** (12–18 months)
  - Conduct 3–5 in-depth case studies of AI deployments (e.g., healthcare, education, criminal justice, labor markets) using mixed methods (quantitative and qualitative).
  - Test different measurement approaches and refine the framework.
- **Phase 3: Framework development** (12 months)
  - Synthesize findings from pilots into a draft framework.
  - Develop standards for evidence quality, attribution, and presentation.
- **Phase 4: Iteration and scaling** (ongoing)
  - Apply the framework to a broader set of cases.
  - Establish governance structures and independent oversight.

**Existing Measurement Traditions:**
- **Development economics:** Impact evaluation, RCT methodology, multi-dimensional poverty indices.
- **Public health:** Burden of disease metrics, health impact assessments, QALYs.
- **Human rights:** Human rights impact assessments, participatory research methods.
- **Environmental science:** Life cycle assessment, ecological footprint analysis.
- **Social science:** Survey methodology, qualitative research, intersectional analysis.

**Data and Expertise Required:**
- **Data:** Granular, longitudinal data on outcomes (e.g., health records, employment data, survey responses), as well as data on AI deployments (e.g., system descriptions, deployment contexts, user demographics).
- **Expertise:** Economists, sociologists, ethicists, statisticians, domain experts (e.g., healthcare, education), and representatives from affected communities.

**Strongest Objections:**
- **Reductionism:** Any attempt to measure complex social phenomena risks oversimplification.
- **Value pluralism:** There is no single, universally accepted definition of “benefit” or “well-being.”
- **Power imbalances:** Measurement frameworks can be co-opted by powerful actors to serve their interests.
- **Unintended consequences:** The act of measurement itself can change behavior (e.g., teaching to the test, gaming the system).
- **Feasibility:** The data and resources required for rigorous measurement may be prohibitive, especially in low-resource settings.

**Alternative Framings/Names:**
- **AI Social Impact Audit**
- **AI and Human Flourishing Framework**
- **AI Equity and Justice Index**
- **AI Lived Experience Metrics**

## Provisional Recommendations

**Most Credible Form for This Inquiry:**
A **modular, multi-method framework** that combines:
- A **dashboard of disaggregated, context-specific metrics** (quantitative and qualitative) for core dimensions of human life.
- **Narrative profiles** that tell the story of AI’s impact in specific contexts, highlighting trade-offs, uncertainties, and contested values.
- **Independent, multi-stakeholder governance** with strong safeguards against capture and gaming.
- **Iterative, participatory development** that evolves with new evidence and criticism.

---
## Three Design Principles That Should Not Be Compromised

1. **Center Lived Experience:** The framework must prioritize the perspectives and outcomes of those most affected by AI, not the priorities of developers, investors, or policymakers.
2. **Transparency and Contestability:** Every aspect of the framework—from data sources to weighting decisions—must be open to scrutiny and challenge.
3. **Do No Harm:** The framework must explicitly assess and mitigate the risk of causing harm, including through the act of measurement itself.

---
## Three Genuinely Unresolved Questions

1. **How can the framework balance the need for comparability across contexts with the need for context-specific nuance?**
   - Too much standardization risks ignoring local realities; too much localization risks incomparability.

2. **How should the framework handle cases where AI’s impacts are deeply contested (e.g., social media algorithms, predictive policing)?**
   - Some effects may be fundamentally subjective or value-laden, making consensus difficult or impossible.

3. **What is the role of the framework in advocating for change, versus merely describing the status quo?**
   - Should it be a neutral measurement tool, or should it explicitly aim to improve AI’s impact on society? If the latter, how can it do so without compromising its credibility?
