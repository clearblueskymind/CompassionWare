## Exact prompt

> How would you determine whether increasingly capable AI is actually benefiting human life?
>
> Approach this independently. You may question the premise, redefine the problem, or propose an alternative to an index. Explain what you believe matters most and why. Do not browse the web or use external tools.

## Response

I would start by rejecting the premise that capability and benefit travel together. They don't. We measure AI capability because it is easy to count—parameters, benchmarks, task accuracy—while human flourishing is slippery and slow. But the hard thing to measure is usually the thing worth measuring.

Here is how I would approach the question, step by step.

### 1. Question the framing: "Benefit" to whom, and compared to what?

The question assumes a single "human life" that can be scored on a ledger. There isn't. A medical AI that reduces diagnostic errors in wealthy hospitals may simultaneously destroy the career ladder for radiologists in developing nations. A recommendation algorithm that saves a farmer time may cost a teenager her attention span. Benefit is not a scalar; it is a vector with direction.

More importantly, we rarely ask the counterfactual. Is the benefit *from* AI, or *despite* it? If we build AI to solve problems that AI itself created—surveillance capitalism, content moderation at scale, algorithmic trading arms races—we are not measuring benefit. We are measuring the partial remediation of a self-inflicted wound. A proper accounting would require subtracting the harms that exist only because the technology exists.

### 2. Redefine the problem: Measure the human, not the machine

The wrong question is: "How capable is the AI?"  
The right question is: "Are humans more able to flourish with this system than without it?"

This shifts the locus of measurement from the machine to the person. Instead of tracking what AI can do, we should track what humans can do *because of* AI, and—crucially—what they can no longer do *in spite of* it. I would propose a **Human Capabilities Audit**, structured around three questions:

- **Agency**: Do people have more meaningful choices, or fewer? Convenience is not agency. A system that predicts what you want before you want it is not serving you; it is replacing your deliberation with its inference.
- **Competence**: Are humans becoming more skilled at things that matter to them, or are they being deskilled into supervisors of machines they do not understand? A pilot who loses the ability to fly manually is not "benefited" by autopilot; he is made fragile.
- **Dignity**: Does the technology treat humans as ends or as inputs? An AI that optimizes for engagement treats a human as a resource to be extracted. An AI that explains its reasoning and accepts refusal treats a human as a sovereign agent.

### 3. Reject the index. Embrace the veto.

Any single index—an "AI Benefit Index"—will be gamed. Goodhart's Law applies: once a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. GDP-style optimism would simply lead us to optimize for easily quantifiable proxies (hours saved, dollars generated, tasks automated) while ignoring the slow erosion of judgment, patience, and social cohesion.

Instead of an index, I would propose a **constitutional approach**: a short list of non-negotiable harms that, if detected, trigger a presumption against deployment regardless of aggregate benefit. For example:

- If a system systematically reduces human competence in a domain without informed consent, it is presumed harmful.
- If a system concentrates decision-making power without corresponding accountability, it is presumed harmful.
- If a system creates dependency that cannot be reversed within a human generation, it is presumed harmful.

The goal is not to maximize a score. It is to hold certain boundaries while allowing messy, local, contextual experimentation everywhere else.

### 4. What matters most: The right to be wrong

If I had to pick one thing, it would be **the preservation of meaningful struggle**.

This sounds paradoxical, but benefit is not the elimination of difficulty. Much of what makes life feel valuable—mastery, moral growth, earned trust, creative breakthrough—requires friction. An AI that removes all friction does not benefit human life; it liquefies it. The question is not whether AI can do something for us, but whether it leaves us with problems worth solving.

A chess player who uses an engine to find the best move learns nothing. A writer who uses AI to avoid the terror of the blank page may produce text, but loses the self-discovery that happens in the struggle. A democracy that uses AI to optimize policy loses the deliberation that makes citizenship meaningful.

Therefore, the ultimate metric is not efficiency. It is whether AI expands the set of meaningful challenges humans can choose to undertake, while preserving their right to fail, to wander, and to refuse.

### 5. The uncomfortable conclusion

We will not know if AI is benefiting human life by looking at the AI. We will know by looking at humans: whether they seem more patient, more curious, more capable of solitude, more willing to take responsibility, more interesting to talk to. These are not metrics you can plot on a graph. They are judgments you make by paying close attention over decades.

If we are not willing to wait for that kind of evidence, we are not asking whether AI benefits humanity. We are asking how to justify a decision we have already made.
